E-commerce cash flow

How do you pay for production while Amazon holds your funds?

By ProofNotFluff, run by a finance executive · Worked example from the free Amazon DD+7 payout calculator's model. Checked Nov 6, 2026.

The short answer

Treat the hold as a cash gap you fund once, then grow only as fast as that fund allows. The gap is roughly what you spend on production each day times the days from order to money in your bank. Request your payout every day to shorten those days, and push production bills later where a supplier or a card you clear each month allows.

For a seller paid $1,200 a day with $600 a day going to production, that comes to $7,200 with a daily payout request and $13,200 on the 14-day payout. Each extra $100 a day of sales needs another $600 to $1,100 up front, which is why a good sales week can leave the bank account empty.

The example uses round numbers from a seller's post in the Amazon Seller Forums: about $1,200 a day in sales, about half of it going to production, and funds held 15 days or more. It treats $1,200 as the amount Amazon pays out a day and $600 a day as the production spend, with no other costs and $0 of spare cash at the start. Everything else is the free DD+7 calculator's example: fulfilled by Amazon, delivered 3 days after the order, the next 14-day payout 14 days away, and Express Payout landing 1 day after release. Following Amazon's own example, money becomes available the day after the 7-day reserve ends.

With a daily request, as long as each day's payout covers that day's production, the cash needed depends only on the production spend and the days, not on the size of the payout: nothing arrives until day 13, so 12 days of production go out first. On the 14-day payout, Amazon's referral and FBA fees make each real payout smaller than sales, so the real figure is higher than the one shown.

How to close the gap

  1. Work out your own number

    Take what you spend on production per day and multiply it by the days from order to bank. With a daily request that is $600 times 12 days, or $7,200. Look up your own days in Seller Central: the Deferred filter in your transactions lists each held order and the date Amazon expects to release it.

  2. Request your payout every day

    Disburse on Demand lets you request your available balance once every 24 hours, so money no longer waits for the next 14-day payout day. In the example it cuts the cash needed by $6,000. In the forum thread, sellers disagreed on whether every account has the button; one with an account from 2020 said it works. Check the Payments page in your own account.

  3. Get production billed later

    The first reply in the thread suggests trade accounts with suppliers that give 30 days to pay. If a production bill falls due 30 days after the sales it made, and money reaches your bank 12 days after each order, the payout lands before the bill does. The asker said their suppliers do not offer terms, so ask again once you have a record of paying on time.

  4. Use a card only if you clear it

    Two replies suggest putting production on a credit card, paid down as payouts land. One adds a limit: only if it is paid in full every month. A balance carried past the due date usually costs interest.

  5. Grow in steps your cash covers

    Every extra $100 a day of sales needs about $600 more on hand with a daily request, and $1,100 on the 14-day payout. Before a promotion or a bigger order, set that cash aside first. The asker turns on vacation mode when sales rise; one reply says to let stock run out instead.

  6. Keep valid tracking on orders you ship

    Without valid tracking from an integrated carrier, Amazon waits for the latest estimated delivery date. In the example that stretches order to bank from 12 to 16 days and lifts the cash needed to $9,600.

Why more sales leave you with less cash

In the example, production goes out the day you sell, and the money for that sale comes back 12 days or more later. Sell more and more goes out before anything comes back. In the example, a jump from $1,200 to $1,800 a day needs $3,600 more with a daily request and $6,600 more on the 14-day payout. The extra cash comes back only when sales fall again, so a business that keeps growing keeps needing more.

This is the gap a profitable seller can still fall into. One reply in the thread puts it plainly: profitable businesses still fail when cash runs short.

When Amazon holds more than 7 days

Amazon can extend the 7-day reserve period after a review of risk and past performance, and can hold an account level reserve for A-to-z claims, chargebacks or an account review. One reply says DD+7 applies whatever your history. If your Deferred list shows release dates well past delivery plus 7, use your own days in the sum above.

If the gap is bigger than your cash

Replies in the thread suggest a business line of credit or a loan, and one warns against business loans when starting out, since terms tend to be short and rates high for new businesses. Any borrowing has a cost, so set its terms against your own numbers before you sign. This page is not financial advice.

Questions

How much cash do I need while Amazon holds my funds?

About your daily production spend times the days from order to bank. In the example, $600 a day needs $7,200 with a daily payout request and $13,200 on the 14-day payout.

Why do I run out of cash when sales go up?

Production for the extra sales goes out now and the payout comes back later. In the example, each extra $100 a day of sales needs $600 to $1,100 more cash before payouts catch up.

How long does Amazon hold funds?

Under DD+7, until 7 days after delivery, then the money waits for your payout and your bank. In the example that is 12 days from order to bank with a daily request and 12 to 25 days on the 14-day payout.

Can I get paid by Amazon every day?

Disburse on Demand lets you request your available balance once every 24 hours. It skips the wait for the payout day, not the 7-day reserve.

Is this financial advice?

No. It is a planning estimate from round numbers and Amazon's published rules.
