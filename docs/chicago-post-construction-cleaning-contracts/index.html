What a final clean usually covers

Builders work from the project's specifications, which usually include a final cleaning section. Here is what one public university's construction standard asks for, as an example of the scope a builder will expect you to quote.

AreaWhat the spec asks for
SiteRubbish, waste, litter and other debris removed
Hard surfacesInterior and exterior finishes brought to a dirt-free condition
Hidden spacesDebris and dust removed from roofs, plenums, shafts, attics and similar spaces
FloorsHard floors vacuumed and mopped; carpet vacuumed, and cleaned to the manufacturer's recommendations if soil or stains remain
Glass and mirrorsCleaned and polished, glazing compounds removed
Fixtures and equipmentPlumbing fixtures cleaned to a sanitary condition; light fixtures and equipment wiped down
Air systemDisposable filters replaced, permanent filters cleaned, diffusers, registers and grilles cleaned

Source: Austin Peay State University construction standard, section 01 77 00.1 Final Cleaning. Every project's own spec governs; ask the builder for the cleaning section before you price.

How to do it

  1. Find the builder on the permit

    Look up the address in the City of Chicago's Building Permits data on data.cityofchicago.org. Each permit lists its contacts by role, and the contact marked as general contractor is the builder running the job.

  2. Ask to get on the bidders list

    Write to the builder's main office and ask to be added to its bidders list for cleaning. Include your insurance, licenses, references and examples of past work, as the trade magazine Cleaning & Maintenance Management advises.

  3. Have your insurance ready before you bid

    General contractors usually insist that any vendor on their site carries insurance, workers' compensation is usually required once you have employees, and some clients ask for a surety bond, per ZenBusiness. Commercial clients can pay on net 30 or net 60 terms, so plan your cash for the wait.

  4. Start with builders who have many jobs open

    A builder with several open jobs may buy cleaning for more than one of them. On this Monday's list, Skender Construction LLC has 23 open projects, Executive Construction, Inc. has 23 and JC Anderson Inc has 23.

  5. Call about one named project

    Ask who buys cleaning for that project. Have the address and the permit number ready, and send a one-page capability sheet the same day: what you clean, crew size, insurance, equipment and two references.

  6. Ask for a small first job

    Offer one floor, one phase or the rough clean on a smaller project. A small job is a smaller decision for a builder that has never used you, and good work there can lead to a reference.

  7. Get in before the end of the job

    Reach the builder while the job is still under way, not after it closes. A project whose permit shows partial occupancy approved is near the end. This week's count at that stage: 17.

  8. Walk the site only to introduce yourself

    At the job trailer, ask for the superintendent or project manager, introduce yourself, leave a card and go, as Cleaning & Maintenance Management suggests. Wear a hard hat and a safety vest, and stay out of the way of the work.

  9. Get each phase signed off

    When you clean in stages, ask the superintendent to sign off each unit or phase as complete. Pricing each unit and phase on its own makes invoicing simpler and gives you a clear basis to charge for a re-clean.

Questions

Where do I find the general contractor for a Chicago building?

In the City of Chicago's Building Permits data on data.cityofchicago.org. Each permit lists contacts with their relationship to the permit, and a contact marked CONTRACTOR-GENERAL CONTRACTOR is the builder.

How do I find post-construction cleaning jobs near me?

Start from building permits, which are public. In Chicago, open permits for large commercial and apartment projects name the builder, and this week 1,129 of them pass our filter. Contact the builder about a named project.

What does post-construction cleaning include?

It depends on the project's spec. A typical final cleaning section covers debris removal, hard surfaces, floors, glass, fixtures, light fixtures and the air system's filters and grilles. Ask the builder for the cleaning section before you price.

Is this the same data I can get free from the City?

Yes, the source is the City's public data, and anyone can search it. The paid list saves the filtering: of the 3,069 permits we pulled this week we keep 1,129, open commercial and apartment projects of $500,000 or more with a business named as general contractor.

Does a project on the list need a cleaner?

Not necessarily. A permit shows that work is planned or under way and who the builder is. It does not say who cleans the job or whether that work is already taken. Ask the builder.

Do you contact builders for me?

No. You make your own calls and emails. We never contact anyone on your behalf, and no business listed has endorsed or works with ProofNotFluff.
