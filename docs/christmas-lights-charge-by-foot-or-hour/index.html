Christmas light pricing

Charge by the foot or by the hour to hang Christmas lights, and who should own the lights?

By ProofNotFluff, run by a finance executive · Worked example from the free Christmas light installation pricing calculator. Checked Oct 29, 2026.

The short answer

Quote by the foot, but work the foot price out from your hours. The customer gets one price before you climb the ladder, and the price stays the same whether your crew is quick or slow, so getting faster is yours to keep. In the thread this page answers, the two replies that gave a method both priced by the foot, and Service Autopilot's guide for installers says most businesses do.

On the lights, lending them (a seasonal rental you store and keep up) pays least in the first season and most after. In the worked example, the same 150-foot house quoted at $915 every season pays about $49 for each person-hour on the job in the season you buy the lights and about $84 in the seasons after. Hanging a customer's own lights is the easiest way to start and, by the thread's account, the one most installers drop.

How to decide, step by step

  1. Price from your hours, quote by the foot

    Time your crew on a few houses this season. Turn the hours, drive and lights into a job price in the calculator, then divide by the feet of roofline. That foot price is what you quote; the hours are how you check it still pays.

  2. Keep the hourly price for jobs you cannot measure

    A tall tree, a custom wrap or a repair call on strands you did not hang is hard to price by the foot. A time charge with a written estimate keeps a slow surprise from coming out of your pay.

  3. Pick one light plan to start

    Lending means buying stock and storing it, so the first season pays less. In the example, buying the lights for one 150-foot house costs $360 up front. If cash is tight, start by selling the lights or hanging the customer's own, and move to lending as the route grows.

  4. Hold the price in later seasons

    The company in the thread keeps its per foot price the same every year so later seasons pay for the first. It said that if it dropped the price after year one, the first-year price would have to be more like $6 a foot.

  5. Write down what the price covers

    Say whether the price includes takedown, storage, swapping dead bulbs and service calls. The company in the thread lists custom fitting, off-season storage, upkeep and free service calls with its rental, and asks for no contract.

What the thread says

The question was posted Nov 1, 2020 on Window Cleaning Resource by a window cleaner whose customers had asked for holiday lights and who had only ever hung lights at their own house. Their customers owned their lights. They asked whether to charge by the length or by the difficulty and time.

One reply said most providers charge per foot, and that installing customer-supplied lights is common at the start but most stop once they have enough clients, calling it a headache that kills morale. A window cleaning company replied that it charges $4 to $5 a foot depending on how hard the job is, with the lights rented from the company. It said it expects roughly $30 to $50 an hour in year one, when it buys the lights, and $70 to $100 an hour in later years. Later in the thread it said it offers only C9 strands and is moving toward $4 a foot on residential work.

Why the first season pays less when you lend

When you lend, you pay for the lights in season 1 but the customer pays the same price every season. In the example the $360 of lights comes out of the first season's $915, so that season pays about $49 a person-hour; with the lights paid for, seasons 2 and 3 pay about $84. That is the same shape as the company in the thread describes, from different inputs. Selling the lights flips it: the customer pays $1,220 in season 1 (about $81 a person-hour) and $710 after (about $66).

Over 3 seasons, lending brings in $2,745 and $829.41 of profit in the example, against $2,640 and $799.41 when the customer buys, and you still hold the stock. Lights that last longer than 3 seasons, with the price held at $915, make lending pay more again.

The customer's own lights

Hanging lights the customer already owns needs no stock and no storage, which is why it is the usual start. In the example it quotes $710 and pays about $66 a person-hour. Service Autopilot's guide calls it one of the least reliable choices: the strands may not work, and a customer can blame you for damage. If you take these jobs, test the strands before you hang them and price repairs by the hour.

What the guides say about lending and selling

Service Autopilot's guide for installers calls renting your own lights one of the most common practices and advises against selling new strands to customers unless you are in a pinch. Nextdoor's guide for businesses describes three plans (lend, sell, or offer both) and says a full service with your lights, setup, takedown and storage can charge more than labor on the customer's own lights. Fixr, a homeowner cost guide, puts hired installation at about $60 to $100 an hour. These are other people's figures, read Oct 29, 2026.

Questions

Should I charge by the foot or by the hour to hang Christmas lights?

By the foot for most houses, worked out from your hours. The customer gets a fixed price and a faster crew earns more. In the example the same $915 pays about $54 a person-hour at 25 feet an hour and $88 at 60. Keep a time charge for tall trees, custom work and repairs.

Should I rent my lights to customers or have them buy the lights?

Lending pays less the first season and more after, and you keep the stock. In the example it pays about $49 a person-hour in season 1 and $84 after. Selling pays more up front, $1,220 in season 1, and the customer keeps the lights.

Should I hang lights the customer already owns?

It is the easy way to start, with no stock to buy or store. One reply in the thread said most stop once they have enough clients. Test the strands first; in the example the labor-only quote is $710.

Should the price stay the same after the first year?

If you lend the lights, yes, by the thread's account: the company that rents its lights keeps the price the same so later years pay for year one. In the example lent lights quote $915 every season.
