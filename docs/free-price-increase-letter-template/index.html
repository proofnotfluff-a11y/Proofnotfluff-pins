The letters

Version 1: Standard notice. For most customers. Short, clear, and it answers the questions people ask first: how much, from when, and what stays the same.

[Your business name]

[Phone] | [Email]

[Date]

Dear [Customer first name],

Thank you for choosing [Your business name] for your [service, for example every-other-week house cleaning].

Starting [effective date], the price of your [service] will change from [old price] to [new price] per [visit, month or job]. Everything else stays the same: the same [team, day and checklist].

We review our prices [once a year] so we can keep paying our team fairly and keep doing the work the way you expect. [Optional: This is our first price change since (year).]

Any [visits] before [effective date] stay at your current price. If you have questions, call or text me at [phone] or reply to this [email].

Thank you for your business.

[Your name]

[Your business name]

Version 2: Long-time customer

For customers who have been with you for years. It thanks them first and gives them something for staying: a later start date or a small loyalty discount.

[Your business name]

[Phone] | [Email]

[Date]

Dear [Customer first name],

You have been with us since [year], and we don't take that for granted. [Optional: a personal thank-you, for example: Thank you for always making room for us on short notice.]

I wanted you to hear this from me first. Starting [effective date], the price of your [service] will go from [old price] to [new price] per [visit]. [Optional: It is the first change to your price in (number) years.]

Because you have been with us so long, [pick one and delete the other: your new price won't start until (later date) / we are keeping a (dollar amount) loyalty discount on your new price].

Nothing else changes. Same [team], same [day], same care.

If you would like to talk it over, call me at [phone]. Thank you for [number] years.

[Your name]

[Your business name]

Version 3: Rising costs

When one cost, such as fuel, supplies, insurance or labor, is the reason. It names the cost and shows the change in dollars.

[Your business name]

[Phone] | [Email]

[Date]

Dear [Customer first name],

I'm writing to let you know about a change to our prices, and the reason for it.

Over the past [12 months], what we pay for [supplies, fuel, insurance or labor] has gone up. [Optional: name the cost, for example: Our fuel bill went from about (old amount) to (new amount) a month.] We have covered the difference ourselves until now, but to keep [the same products, crew and schedule] on every visit, we need to adjust.

Starting [effective date], your [service] will be [new price] per [visit], up from [old price]. That is [difference] more per [visit].

[Optional: If (cost) comes back down, we will look at our prices again in (month).]

Questions? Call or text me at [phone]. Thank you for sticking with us.

[Your name]

[Your business name]

How to send it

  1. Pick the new price first. The free price increase calculator shows the price that keeps your profit or your margin after your costs rise, and how many customers you can lose before you earn less than today.
  2. Pick a version. Standard notice for most customers, long-time customer for people who have been with you for years, rising costs when one cost is the reason.
  3. Fill every bracket. In the Word file, search for [ to find each field. Delete any Optional line you don't use, and the version box at the top of the page.
  4. Pick how much notice to give. These are choices, not rules. Pick one and use the same date for every customer who gets the same letter.
  5. Send it the way you bill. Email it to customers you invoice by email. Print it and leave it with the invoice for customers you see in person. A short text can point to the email. Send every copy on the same day.
  6. Keep a record. Note the date you sent each letter in your client list, so you can answer "when did you tell me?" later.

How much notice: choices, not rules

Notice periods owners pick, and when
NoticeWhen owners pick it
2 weeksShort. Some owners use it for one-time or on-call work, where there is no regular schedule to plan around.
30 daysAbout one billing cycle for customers you bill monthly, so the new price lands on the next full month.
60 days or moreMore time to plan. Some owners give this to long-time customers or customers on a set schedule.
At renewalIf you sell seasonal or yearly plans, change the price when the plan renews instead of in the middle of it.

If a customer has a written agreement with you, check what it says about price changes before you pick a date. This page doesn't say what any law requires, and rules can vary by place and by contract.

Questions

What should a price increase letter say?

The new price, the old price, the date it starts, and what stays the same. A short reason helps, and so does a way to reach you with questions. Each letter in the template covers those in five or six short lines.

How much notice should I give before raising prices?

The template lists common choices, not rules: about two weeks for one-time or on-call work, about 30 days or one billing cycle for regular customers, 60 days or more for long-time customers, or at renewal for seasonal and yearly plans. If a customer has a written agreement with you, check what it says first. This page doesn't say what any law requires.

Should I give a reason for the increase?

That is up to you. The standard letter gives a general one. The rising costs letter names the cost and shows the change in dollars, which can help when one cost, such as fuel or supplies, clearly went up.
