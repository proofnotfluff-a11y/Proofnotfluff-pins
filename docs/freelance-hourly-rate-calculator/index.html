How it works

The gap between the rate a freelancer charges and the pay they keep comes from two places: the hours no client pays for (pitching, email, invoicing, learning) and the tax and costs that come out before anything is yours.

This calculator grosses your take-home up for the tax share you set aside, adds a year of business expenses, and spreads that revenue over the hours a client actually pays for: your working hours times your billable share. That is your minimum hourly rate; the rate card rounds it up to the next $5.

To test a rate, it multiplies the rate by your billable hours, takes out expenses and your tax set-aside on any profit, and divides what is left by every hour you work. A project quote is its billable hours at your rate-card rate, plus any project costs with your markup.

A worked example

A $60,000 take-home with a 25% tax set-aside and $6,000 of expenses needs $86,000 of revenue. At 48 weeks of 40 hours with 60% billable, that is 1,152 billable hours out of 1,920 worked, so the minimum rate is $74.65 (rate card $75). Charging $40 an hour brings in $46,080 and leaves $30,060 after expenses and tax: $15.66 for every hour worked, $29,940 short of the goal. A 12-hour project at $75 is $900 of labor; $150 of project costs with a 15% markup adds $172.50, for a $1,072.50 quote. Billing 80% of your hours instead of 60% would bring the minimum rate down to $55.99.

Questions

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?

Start from the take-home you want, add the tax you set aside and a year of business expenses, then divide by the hours clients actually pay for, not every hour you work. With the example numbers that is $86,000 over 1,152 billable hours, or $74.65 an hour.

Why is my rate so much higher than my old salary divided by hours?

A salary comes with paid time off, no unbilled admin and an employer who covers the business costs. As a freelancer you pay those from your rate, and only the billable share of your week earns it.

What billable share should I use?

Track a few weeks of your own time and divide the hours a client paid for by all hours worked. The example's 60% is a starting point, not a benchmark. The table under the results shows the rate you would need at 50%, 60%, 70% and 80% billed.

How do I turn the rate into a project price?

Estimate the billable hours the project takes, multiply by your rate-card rate, and add any costs you pass on with your markup. The example's 12-hour project with $150 of costs quotes at $1,072.50.

What tax set-aside rate should I use?

Ask your tax preparer. A set-aside rate is a savings habit you choose, not a tax calculation, and this calculator does not tell you which rate to use.

Is this tax or financial advice?

No. It is a planning estimate based on the numbers you enter.
