Lead cost

Google Local Services Ads cost me about $500 a month and about half the leads are small jobs. Are they still worth it?

By ProofNotFluff, run by a finance executive · Worked example from the free cost per lead calculator. Google's Local Services Ads help pages read Nov 1, 2026.

The short answer

Only if the jobs they win leave more than they cost. Judge them per won job, not per lead: divide the month's bill by the jobs you actually won, and set that against what one job leaves after labor and supplies. If the lead cost per won job is higher, the ads lost money that month, however cheap each lead looked.

At $500 a month and the free calculator's example job ($280 with $110 of direct cost, so $170.00 left), the bill takes 3 won jobs to cover. Turned down leads still cost you: Google charges for each valid lead, such as a call you answer, and no longer gives credits for leads for job types you don't service. If half your leads are jobs you refuse, each lead you can use costs double: $30.00 becomes $60.00 in the example. Before you cancel, turn off the job types you don't want and set a max per lead bid.

From the free cost per lead calculator's example month: $1,200 paid for 40 leads, 18 quotes sent and 6 jobs won at $280 each, with $110 of labor, supplies and gas per job. That is $30.00 a lead, $66.67 a quote and $200.00 in leads for each job won. The jobs brought in $1,680 and cost $660 to do, leaving $1,020, so after the $1,200 lead bill the month lost $180. Covering that bill took 8 won jobs, not 6.

If 20 of the 40 leads were jobs you turned down, the same $1,200 bought 20 leads you could use, at $60.00 each. These are the calculator's example numbers, a cleaning business's month, not the asker's: type your own bill, leads and jobs into the calculator.

How to tell if the ads pay, step by step

  1. Count one month

    From the Leads page in your Local Services Ads account and your own books: what you were charged, how many leads, how many you quoted and how many jobs you won.

  2. Work out what a job leaves

    Average job price less the labor, supplies and gas you spend only because you did it. In the example, $280 less $110 leaves $170.00.

  3. Set lead cost per won job against it

    Divide the bill by the jobs won. In the example, $1,200 and 6 jobs is $200.00 a won job, more than the $170.00 a job leaves, so each job came out $30.00 short after its lead.

  4. Turn off the job types you don't want

    Google's help pages say to refine service areas, categories and service types in your settings if you get unwanted leads, with each job type toggled on or off by industry. Turning off every job type does not stop leads for the overall category; only pausing the ad does.

  5. Cap what a lead can cost

    With Max per lead bidding you set the most you'd pay for a lead. Google says it takes about 2 weeks to see accurate results. Rate each lead in the account: Google says the feedback helps it match leads. Low quality leads can be credited automatically.

What the thread says

The question was posted May 3, 2026 on Pressure Washing Resource by an owner charged about $500 a month for Google Local Services Ads, who said this year brought lower end calls and that about half of them were turned down, such as cleaning only one side or a walkway for 10 minutes. The owner was thinking of cancelling, and later added that word of mouth was enough.

One reply, from a newer washer, reported one message at a $30 cost in 2 months, a recommended budget of $270 a week in a rural home county that went unused, while the recommended budget for the busier county next door was $3,500 a week. Another reply said not to use Local Services Ads because you can't add negative keywords, and to run a search campaign with long, specific keywords instead, saying a click on driveway cleaning may cost $20 or more where a more specific phrase may cost $2 to $3.

What Google charges for, and what it credits

You pay for valid leads, not clicks. Google's help page lists what counts, such as a call you answer or return and speak with the customer, a text or email, or a voicemail. A lead's price can vary with your location, the job type, the type of lead and your bidding mode, and follow up contact with the same customer within 15 days is not charged again.

Leads judged invalid or low quality are not charged, and charged leads can be credited automatically if Google's models later find them low quality, in most cases within 30 days. Google says it no longer supports credits for job type not serviced and geo not serviced leads. So the fix for small jobs is in your settings, not in disputes.

How the weekly budget works

Your average weekly budget is a target. Some weeks spend less and some more, but you never pass the monthly max, which Google sets at the weekly budget times 30.4, divided by 7. A $270 weekly budget, the recommended budget one reply gave for a rural county, allows up to $1,172.57 in a month; the asker's $500 a month is about $115.13 a week.

Under Maximize leads, Google sets the bid and recommends a budget of at least 10 leads a week. Max per lead lets you set the bid yourself, which also changes how many leads your weekly budget buys.

When cancelling makes sense

If word of mouth already fills your calendar, as the asker says, and the lead cost per won job stays above what a job leaves after you have changed the job types and the bid, the ads are costing you money. A search campaign, as the thread suggests, charges per click instead of per lead, and the reply that recommends it says the setup can take several days, plus a few hours a month of review.
