Post-construction cleaning pricing

How do you price post-construction cleaning without cutting your price?

By ProofNotFluff, run by a finance executive · Updated Oct 26, 2026 from City of Chicago building permits. Refreshed every Monday.

The short answer

Usually you should not cut it. Price the job from your own costs, not from the client's budget, and when a builder says the quote is too high, change the scope, the schedule or the phases before you touch the rate. A bid under your floor pays you less than your own goal for every hour on site.

One lost bid also tells you little: the builder may already have a cleaner, or a lower number from someone who has not counted their costs. The answer to one lost contract is more bids. In Chicago this week 1,129 open projects of $500,000 or more name a builder you can ask.

Worked example: the floor under one crew day

The free cleaning price per square foot calculator's example business wants $48,000 a year take-home with a 12% income tax estimate, has $505 a month of business costs, and works 48 weeks of 45 hours, cleaning for 70% of them. That gives a break-even rate of $46.98 per clean hour: pay goal, taxes and costs covered, no profit.

Fixr lists 2 workers for 8 hours as a common minimum for post-construction labor, so take one crew day as the job.

Fixr says a worker usually cleans 100 to 200 sq ft an hour in a new construction home, so 16 person-hours covers about 1,600 to 3,200 sq ft, and $885 lands inside Fixr's $0.20 to $0.60 range for a final clean. Price the area you will actually clean, not the building's gross size.

The $885 bid works out to $55.31 per person-hour, above the $30 to $50 per worker Fixr's home guide lists. If the buyers you quote pay less than your floor, the fix is a faster crew, a smaller scope or a different buyer, not a price under the floor.

Drive time, supplies, debris hauling and windows are left out to keep the sum short; add your own. The margin works as in the calculator: the floor divided by 0.85. Swap in your own numbers in the calculator before you quote.

What to trim instead of the price

The reply marked as the solution in the forum thread that asked this question put it simply: a big contract that does not cover its costs is a liability, so adjust the scope, the timeline or the package instead of the price. Here is what that can look like on a construction clean.

OptionWhat changes
Split the phasesQuote rough, final and touch-up cleaning as separate lines, so the builder can buy the final clean alone
Drop the outsideFixr prices external work (outside windows, debris, pressure washing) as its own line; leave it to another trade or a later visit
Move the debrisFixr lists debris removal at $200 to $600 as a separate cost; the builder's own dumpster and laborers may already cover it
Stretch the scheduleA wider window lets a smaller crew do the same scope over more days
Start with one phaseOffer one floor or one phase at your full rate, so the builder can test you on a smaller decision

Each option lowers the total by taking work out, while your rate per hour stays the same.

For commercial work, ThePricer (updated Oct 8, 2026, citing a 2026 commercial cleaning rate guide) lists rough cleaning at $0.10 to $0.30 per sq ft, final cleaning at $0.30 to $0.75 and touch-up at $0.10 to $0.20. If your price sits above the range, check your speed and scope before you check your rate.

How to price the next bid so you can hold it

  1. Know your floor first

    Work out your break-even rate per clean hour from your pay goal, taxes and business costs. That number, times the hours a job takes, is the lowest price that still pays you.

  2. Get the cleaning section of the spec

    Ask the builder for the project's final cleaning specification and walk the site. Price what the spec asks for, not a guess at a typical job.

  3. Count person-hours by phase

    Estimate rough, final and touch-up time separately, with crew size. Time on your past jobs is the best guide, or Fixr's 100 to 200 sq ft per worker-hour if you have none; the calculator's speed field takes it.

  4. Price each phase as its own line

    A line-by-line quote shows the builder what each part costs and gives you room to remove a part without cutting your rate.

  5. Add your margin last

    Divide the floor by one minus your margin, then compare the total per square foot with the going rates above.

  6. Ask which line is too high

    When the builder pushes back, ask what number they need and what they compared you with. Then offer a smaller scope at the same rate.

  7. Walk away below the floor

    If the only way to win is a price under your floor, decline politely and ask to stay on the builder's bidders list for the next job.

  8. Send more bids

    Builders with several open projects have more than one job to clean. Quote the next named project instead of lowering this one.

Is the price too high, or the client wrong for you?

Before you lower a number, check what it was compared with. A builder holding a lower bid may be comparing a full rough, final and touch-up scope with a final clean only, so ask what the other quote covered.

Replies in the forum thread warned against buying a contract with a low price. One cleaner described winning a client with a low price so they would keep coming back, then going unpaid for the final cleaning when the client stopped answering. Another pointed out that a discount teaches a client to expect the next one. A deliberate lower margin can make sense for a clear reason, such as a first job with a builder who has many projects, but that is a choice you make with your floor in view, not a cut under it.

Questions

How much should I charge per square foot for post-construction cleaning?

Start from your own floor: your break-even rate per clean hour times the hours the job takes, plus your margin. A worker usually cleans 100 to 200 sq ft an hour in a new construction home, per Fixr. Then compare with published ranges: Fixr lists $0.20 to $0.60 per sq ft for a final clean and $0.15 to $0.40 for a rough clean.

Should I lower my price to win a big contract?

Not below your floor. Above it, a smaller margin can be a deliberate choice for a clear reason. Otherwise trim the scope, split the phases or change the schedule, and keep your rate.

How do I bid post-construction cleaning jobs?

Get the project's cleaning specification, walk the site, estimate person-hours for each phase, price each phase as a line from your break-even rate, then add your margin.

What do I say when a builder says my quote is too high?

Ask which part is too high and what the other quote covered. Offer a smaller scope, such as the final clean without the outside work, at the same rate.
