Snow removal pricing

Is putting a snow plow on the truck you already own worth it, and what are the pitfalls?

By ProofNotFluff, run by a finance executive · Worked example from the free snow removal pricing calculator and NOAA daily snowfall at O'Hare. Checked Nov 2, 2026.

The short answer

It can be, because the truck is already paid for, so the plow is the main new cost. Whether it pays back turns on two numbers: what each push leaves you after the truck and the salt, and how many pushes a Chicago winter gives you. Work out both before you buy the blade.

In the worked example a driveway prices at $80 a push. Of that, $44.58 is yours: $23.33 for your own 35 minutes and $21.25 on top. At $21.25 a push, every $1,000 you spend on a plow and mount takes 48 pushes to earn back. In a middle winter at O'Hare (6 days with 2 inches or more of new snow), that is 8 driveways for each $1,000; in a light winter it is 12.

From the free calculator's example inputs: 25 minutes on site, 10 minutes of drive from the stop before, 1 person at $40 an hour, the truck and plow at $35 an hour, $15 of salt a visit and a 25% margin. One visit costs $58.75: $23.33 of labor, $20.42 of truck time and $15 of salt. It prices at $80, rounded up to the next $5, so it leaves $21.25.

When you plow yourself, the labor line is your own pay, so a push puts $44.58 in your pocket and the $20.42 of truck time goes to fuel, wear and repairs. The plow payback counts only the $21.25 of profit, so your pay is never spent twice. Your own minutes, truck cost and plow price give your own numbers.

How to decide before you buy the plow

  1. Price one push from your own minutes

    Time a driveway like the ones you would sign, walk included, plus the drive from the stop before. At your labor and truck cost an hour plus salt, that is the cost of a push. Divide by one less your margin for the price: $58.75 at 25% is $80.

  2. Count the pushes a winter gives you

    At O'Hare, the middle of the last 28 winters had 6 days with 2 inches or more of new snow, a light one 4 and a heavy one 8. Set your trigger in writing and use its count.

  3. Work out the plow payback

    Divide the plow, mount and wiring price by your profit a push, then by the pushes in a light winter. In the example each $1,000 takes 48 pushes, or 12 driveways in a light winter.

  4. Call your insurance agent first

    Plowing for pay can change what your policies cover and cost. Ask before the first push, and get the answer in writing.

  5. Sign every driveway

    Put the trigger, the push price, salt and when you get paid in a signed agreement, the same way you would for a roof.

What the thread says

The question was posted Dec 21, 2009 on the RoofersCoffeeShop forum by a roofer in Northern Illinois with an '05 F350 Super Duty 4x4, his main roofing truck. He planned to plow mostly residential driveways and asked whether there was money in it or too much competition, which plow to buy, and which pitfalls to avoid.

One reply came from a plower of about 10 years with 3 trucks and a skid steer, working mostly as a subcontractor for a large landscaping company. He called plowing a very profitable venture if you already own the truck. He quoted $75 to $85 an hour per truck as a subcontractor and about $125 an hour per truck on his own accounts, and suggested a V plow, commercial lots, and the plow brand whose dealer is closest and keeps good hours. In a second reply he said he signs a contract with plow clients the same as with roofing customers, has never had trouble getting paid, and does not work for anyone whose place he cannot find.

The pitfalls owners name

Insurance and claims. A 2016 article for contractors by the accounting firm Withum says plowing can raise general liability and workers compensation premiums, that insurers have begun using standard exclusions, and that claims pile up from damage to the property being plowed and from later slip and fall claims. It says snow contracts put most of the responsibility on the contractor and suggests an attorney read them.

Wear and waiting. The same article names extra wear on equipment and standby time when storms land on weekends or forecasts miss. A 2022 piece by HindSite Software, from its survey of snow businesses, says storms come at any hour, weeks can pass with nothing and then two days run straight, and asks whether you can pay the repairs and equipment insurance a second line of work brings. In the example, $20.42 of every push goes to the truck for that reason.

Driveways or commercial lots

The roofer planned driveways; the reply pointed him to commercial lots, on his own or as a subcontractor. Subcontracting needs no customers of your own, but at $75 an hour the example's driver and truck cost of $75 leaves $0 above your own $40 of pay and the truck. Your own driveways at the example price leave $36.43 an hour after salt and truck, before you count the time to sell and bill them.

HindSite's survey says only 4% of the snow businesses it surveyed did snow removal only, and about 60% said snow was less than 30% of their annual revenue. Most of the businesses it surveyed run snow next to other work, the way the roofer in the thread would.

Questions

Is it worth putting a plow on my truck?

It can be when the truck is already yours. In the example a push at $80 leaves $44.58 for you, and each $1,000 of plow takes 48 pushes of profit to earn back: 8 driveways in a middle Chicago winter, 12 in a light one.

How many pushes does it take to pay back a plow?

At $21.25 of profit a push in the example, each $1,000 of plow, mount and wiring takes 48 pushes. With a push for each day of 2 inches or more at O'Hare, that is 12 driveways in a light winter, 8 in a middle one and 6 in a heavy one.

Should I subcontract or find my own driveways?

Subcontracting needs no customers but pays by the hour. A 2009 forum reply quoted $75 to $85 an hour per truck as a subcontractor and about $125 on its own accounts. At the example's $75 an hour for driver and truck, $75 leaves $0 above your own pay and the truck.

What should I check before my first push?

Call your insurance agent about plowing for pay, sign an agreement with the trigger, price and payment terms, and know where the nearest plow dealer is and its hours during storms.
