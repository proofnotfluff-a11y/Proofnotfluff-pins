# Legal and platform rules for a faceless AI affiliate and AI UGC operation (US, as of October 13, 2026)

Scope note: planning information, not legal advice. Items marked "Attorney check" should be confirmed by counsel before launch. Pages without a printed date are cited as "accessed Oct 13, 2026". The shared web-search budget ran out partway through this research, so some official pages (Meta policy pages, Pinterest policy pages, AI avatar vendor terms, music licensing terms, state laws other than New York) could not be retrieved; those are listed under Gaps, not filled from memory.

## 1. FTC: Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255), Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR Part 465), 2024 to 2026 enforcement, penalties

### Takeaway
An AI avatar presented as a real customer giving a testimonial is a direct violation of the Consumer Review Rule (the FTC states "AI-generated reviews are covered by the final rule"), and an undisclosed affiliate link violates the Endorsement Guides' material-connection rule. Knowing violations of the Rule carry civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation (the 2025 figure still applies; the 2026 inflation adjustment was cancelled). The compliant pattern is: no fabricated persona or experience, disclosure inside the content itself (spoken and on-screen, plus near links), platform tools used in addition to (never instead of) your own disclosure, and substantiation for every claim.

### Cited Findings

**Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (revised; effective July 26, 2023; 88 FR 48092 to 48112, FR Doc. 2023-14795)**
- § 255.0(b): an endorsement is "any advertising, marketing, or promotional message for a product that consumers are likely to believe reflects" the views of a party other than the advertiser; the endorser "could be or appear to be an individual, group, or institution." ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- When a commenter asked the FTC to expressly address "virtual endorsers or fabricated endorsers," the Commission said the definition is intended to cover "the writers of fake reviews and non-existent entities that purport to give endorsements." ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- § 255.0(f): "clearly and conspicuously" means "difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers"; in interactive media (internet, social media) the disclosure "should be unavoidable"; in audiovisual content it should appear in both the visual and audible portions. ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- Example 9(iii) in the revised Guides: an influencer who "relies solely upon a social media platform's built-in disclosure tool" fails, because "The disclosure is easy to miss and thus not clear and conspicuous." ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- Example 10 describes an ad that "discloses via both audio and visual means that the persons featured are actors" (full fact pattern not captured in the excerpt read). Example 11: a disclosure "must be in the same language as the ad," and one that is unreadable on phones fails ("Because some consumers will view the ad on their smartphones, the disclosure is inadequate."). Example 12: "Fake positive reviews that are used to promote a product are" endorsements. ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- § 255.1(c): "the endorser must have been a bona fide user of it at the time the endorsement was given." § 255.1(d): an advertiser "may be liable for a deceptive endorsement even when the endorser is not liable." § 255.1(e): "Endorsers may be liable for statements made in the course of their endorsements." ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- § 255.2(a): "Consumer endorsements themselves are not competent and reliable scientific evidence" (testimonials cannot substitute for claim substantiation). § 255.2 Example 4(v): a manufacturer-procured review is deceptive "because it was not written by a bona fide user of the product." Example 9: paid reviews whose positive nature is required are deceptive "even if the payment is disclosed." ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))
- Material connections must be disclosed when "a significant minority of the audience for an endorsement does not understand or expect the connection"; the preamble uses a blogger paid through "paid affiliate links" as a case requiring clear and conspicuous disclosure. ([Federal Register, July 26, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-07-26/html/2023-14795.htm))

**FTC FAQ, "FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking" (page metadata: last modified July 15, 2025)**
- Affiliate wording: suggested text "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post." "Consumers might not understand that 'affiliate link' means that the person placing the link is getting paid." "'Paid link' right next to an affiliate link should be an adequate disclosure"; "'Commissionable link' is probably not a clear disclosure"; "The closer the disclosure is to your recommendation, the better." ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- Video placement: the disclosure has the "best chance of being clear and conspicuous if it's included in the video itself"; "consumers can easily miss disclosures in the video description"; a visual endorsement needs a disclosure made "at least visually." ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- Platform tools: "just because a platform offers this feature is no guarantee that it's an effective way"; "it's always best to add your own disclosure even if a platform offers its own disclosure tool." ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- Wording: starting a post with "Ad:", "Paid ad", "#ad", "Advertising:" or "Advertisement" "would likely be effective"; "Sponsored by XYZ" is clearer than a bare "Sponsored"; "#ambassador" or "partner" is "ambiguous and confusing"; "Gifted" alone is "likely to be ambiguous." ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- Livestreams: viewers "could easily miss a disclosure at the beginning of the stream"; the FAQ suggests a "continuous, clear and conspicuous disclosure" throughout the stream. ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- Review pages: when the affiliate link is embedded in the review itself, a single disclosure may be adequate; if the review and link are separated, "readers may not make the connection." ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- Liability: "Your company is ultimately responsible for what others do on your behalf"; the FTC's "focus usually will be on advertisers," but "action against an individual endorser might be appropriate in certain circumstances," and "an endorser who isn't an expert may be liable for misleading or unsubstantiated statements." ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))
- The FAQ text retrieved does not address the Amazon Associates sentence or virtual/AI influencers specifically. ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking))

**Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, 16 CFR Part 465 (announced Aug 14, 2024; effective Oct 21, 2024; 89 FR 68077)**
- Announced Aug 14, 2024 on a 5-0 vote; the FTC described the ban as covering reviews and testimonials by people who do not exist, including AI-generated fakes, plus buying sentiment-conditioned reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites, review suppression, and buying or selling fake social media indicators; the rule lets the FTC seek civil penalties against knowing violators. ([FTC press release, Aug 14, 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials))
- Statement of Basis and Purpose: "This rule is effective October 21, 2024." "AI-generated reviews are covered by the final rule" (Section II.A, n.35). On paid actors in a TV ad: "Such conduct would not be covered by the rule unless the underlying testimonials were fake or false"; "an obviously fictional dramatization" is not reasonably seen as an endorsement. Civil penalties reach violators "with actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances." ([Federal Register, Aug 22, 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm))
- § 465.1 definitions (eCFR, up to date as of Oct 8, 2026): consumer review is "a consumer's evaluation, or a purported consumer's evaluation, of a product, service, or business"; a consumer testimonial is a message consumers are likely to believe "reflects the opinions, beliefs, or experiences of a consumer who has purchased, used, or otherwise had experience" with the product; testimonialist is "The individual giving or purportedly giving a consumer testimonial or celebrity testimonial"; fake indicators of social media influence include those "generated by bots, purported individual accounts not associated with a real individual," or hijacked accounts; clear and conspicuous means "easily noticeable (i.e., difficult to miss) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers," and a disclosure the consumer must take an action to see does not qualify. ([eCFR Part 465](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465))
- § 465.2(a): it is a violation to "write, create, or sell" a review or testimonial that "materially misrepresents, expressly or by implication" that the reviewer or testimonialist exists, that they used or otherwise had experience with the product, or their experience. § 465.2(b): purchasing such reviews, or disseminating such testimonials, is a violation where the business "knew or should have known." ([eCFR Part 465](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465))
- § 465.4: no compensation or incentive conditioned on reviews "expressing a particular sentiment, whether positive or negative." § 465.5: insider reviews require clear and conspicuous disclosure of the "material relationship." § 465.6: company-controlled review sites. § 465.7: no "unfounded or groundless legal threat, a physical threat, intimidation, or a public false accusation" to remove reviews, and no misrepresenting that displayed reviews "represent most or all the reviews submitted" when suppressing by rating. § 465.8: no selling or buying fake indicators of social media influence that the party "knew or should have known to be fake" for a commercial purpose. ([eCFR Part 465](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465))

**Enforcement and warning letters, 2024 to 2026**

BCP Director Christopher Mufarrige: "Condemning a technology or service simply because it potentially could be used in a problematic manner" is inconsistent with the law. - Dec 22, 2025: FTC warning letters to 10 companies on the Consumer Review Rule, flagging fake or false reviews and testimonials, sentiment-conditioned incentives, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled review sites, review suppression, and fake social media indicators; example violation in the template: "providing compensation to your employees in exchange for the employee obtaining 5-star reviews from friends and family." ([FTC warning letter template](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2025-Fake-Review-Warning-Template.pdf); [Arnold & Porter, Jan 5, 2026](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/consumer-products-and-retail-navigator/2026/01/ftc-warning-letters-over-consumer-review-rule))

- Arnold & Porter's Jan 2026 summary mentions no other 2025 enforcement under the Rule beyond the warning letters. ([Arnold & Porter, Jan 5, 2026](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/consumer-products-and-retail-navigator/2026/01/ftc-warning-letters-over-consumer-review-rule))

### Inferences
- Liability for AI "customer" avatars sits with whoever makes them: § 465.2(a) reaches anyone who will "write, create, or sell" a testimonial misrepresenting that the testimonialist exists or used the product, with no "should have known" qualifier, and the SBP confirms AI-generated reviews are covered. An operator that deliberately builds AI personas has the knowledge needed for civil penalties. Brands that repost such content face § 465.2(b) exposure. Attorney check: how the FTC would count "violations" (per video, per post, per view) for penalty math.
- The Rytr reversal lowers risk for AI tool vendors, not for people who publish fake reviews or testimonials. Nothing in the Dec 2025 materials narrows § 465.2.
- A brand-voiced AI narrator that does not pretend to be a consumer is not a "consumer testimonial," but when it recommends third-party products for commission it is still an endorsement under Part 255, so the material connection (commission) must be disclosed and every objective claim substantiated (§ 255.2(a): testimonials are not substantiation).
- Disclosure design that satisfies the FTC sources above: say it and show it in the first seconds of the video ("Ad. I earn a commission if you buy through my links."), start the caption with "Ad:" or "#ad", put "(paid link)" beside each link, keep a continuous banner on livestreams, use the same language as the video, and also turn on each platform's toggle. Platform toggles alone fail (Example 9(iii); FAQ).
- If ProofNotFluff ever runs a review page or "best of" list covering a category that includes its own Etsy products, § 465.6 (company-controlled review sites) applies; disclose ownership or exclude its own products. Any request for reviews of its own products must not be conditioned on sentiment (§ 465.4).

## 2. State laws on AI in advertising and synthetic performers

### Takeaway
New York's synthetic performer law (GBL § 396-b, effective June 9, 2026) requires anyone who produces an ad with a human-looking AI performer, with actual knowledge, to "conspicuously disclose" it ($1,000 first violation, $5,000 after). A Dec 11, 2025 federal executive order targets state AI laws but does not suspend this one. Other state laws (California, Illinois, Tennessee and others) could not be verified this pass.

### Cited Findings
- New York S.8420-A/A.8887-B, signed by Gov. Hochul Dec 11, 2025, effective June 9, 2026, amends General Business Law § 396-b. A covered person (one "engaged in the business of 'dealing in any property or service'" who, for a commercial purpose, "produces or creates" an advertisement) must, "where the person has actual knowledge," "conspicuously disclose" that a "synthetic performer is in such advertisement." ([Cooley, Jan 29, 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-29-new-york-enacts-synthetic-performer-disclosure-law-for-advertisements-including-those-using-generative-ai))
- Definition: a "digitally created asset" made or modified by computer, using generative AI or a software algorithm, "intended to create the impression that [it] is engaging in an audiovisual and/or visual performance of a human performer who is not recognizable as any identifiable natural performer." Non-AI software can also create a covered synthetic performer. "Conspicuous" is not defined. ([Cooley, Jan 29, 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-29-new-york-enacts-synthetic-performer-disclosure-law-for-advertisements-including-those-using-generative-ai))
- Exemptions: ads for expressive works (film, TV, streaming, documentaries, video games) where use "is consistent with its use in the underlying work"; audio-only ads; AI used solely to translate a human performer's language; publishers and platforms that only disseminate ads. Penalties: $1,000 first violation, $5,000 each subsequent violation. ([Cooley, Jan 29, 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-29-new-york-enacts-synthetic-performer-disclosure-law-for-advertisements-including-those-using-generative-ai); [Hunton, Dec 19, 2025](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/new-york-enacts-law-regulating-the-use-of-ai-generated-synthetic-performers-in-advertising))
- Venable (via Mondaq, July 20, 2026) confirms the requirement took effect June 9, 2026, says the statute gives no wording or format for the disclosure, suggests following FTC "clear and conspicuous" factors (placement, proximity, size, contrast, duration), and notes AI-enhanced images of real people appear to fall outside the definition but enforcement is untested. ([Mondaq/Venable, July 20, 2026](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/advertising-marketing-branding/1819424/new-york-ai-advertising-disclosure-requirement-now-in-effect))
- The Dec 11, 2025 executive order calls for a "minimally burdensome national policy framework for AI," directs the Attorney General to set up a task force to challenge state AI laws, directs Commerce to publish an evaluation of "onerous" state AI laws by March 2026 (with possible loss of some federal funds), and directs the FTC to issue a policy statement on state laws requiring "alterations to the truthful outputs of AI models." Davis+Gilbert expects legal challenges and advises advertisers to keep complying with state AI laws and prepare for the NY requirement. ([Net Influencer, Dec 19, 2025](https://www.netinfluencer.com/new-york-mandates-ai-disclosure-in-ads-as-white-house-moves-to-block-state-ai-laws/))
- Cooley says the NY law "may face federal opposition" and possible First Amendment litigation. ([Cooley, Jan 29, 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-29-new-york-enacts-synthetic-performer-disclosure-law-for-advertisements-including-those-using-generative-ai))

### Inferences
- A typical "AI UGC" ad (a photoreal AI person who is nobody real, talking to camera about a product) is the exact fact pattern the NY statute defines. The operator always has "actual knowledge," so the disclosure duty attaches whenever the content counts as an advertisement reaching New York. Social video is national by default, so treat this as a nationwide design rule.
- Whether an affiliate creator promoting someone else's product is a person "dealing in" that property or service is not answered by the sources. Attorney check. Low-cost design choice: put "AI-generated presenter" on screen for the whole time a synthetic human appears, plus the platform AI label, whether or not the statute strictly applies.
- A voice-only AI narrator over real product footage likely falls outside the definition (it targets visual or audiovisual performances, and audio-only ads are exempt), but this is untested. Attorney check.
- Penalties are small per violation, but each ad may count separately, and an undisclosed synthetic performer also feeds an FTC deception theory.

## 3. TikTok: AI-generated content labeling, TikTok Shop affiliate and content rules (avatars, LIVE), unoriginal content, commercial content disclosure

### Takeaway
TikTok requires labels on realistic AI content, makes undisclosed commercial content ineligible for the For You feed, treats reuploaded content as ineligible, and, since May 23, 2026, bans AI-generated voices, pre-recorded audio and dominant animated figures in TikTok Shop LIVEs. TikTok reportedly still pays commission on disclosed, non-misleading AI content, but individual brands (SharkNinja) now void commissions for any AI content. TikTok also reduces reach of content that sends US users to buy off-platform.

### Cited Findings
- Current Community Guidelines (document dated Sept 24, 2026, as recorded by the ConductAtlas policy tracker): "We require clear labeling when AI or editing is used to realistically depict people or scenes." "We don't allow AIGC that misleads about matters of public importance or that harms individuals." ([ConductAtlas record of TikTok Community Guidelines, captured Sept 25, 2026](https://conductatlas.com/platform/tiktok/tiktok-community-guidelines/ai-generated-content-labeling-requirement/); official source [tiktok.com/community-guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/))
- Guidelines update effective Sept 13, 2025: the old ban on AIGC showing "fake authoritative sources or crisis events" was replaced with a ban on AIGC "misleading about matters of public importance or harmful to individuals"; the public-figure clause was reworded and the reference to AI endorsements removed; LIVE creators are responsible for third-party tools (real-time translation, voice-to-text) used in their streams; commercial content must be disclosed; TikTok will reduce visibility of content that directs users to "purchase products off-platform in markets where TikTok Shop is available"; For You ineligibility rules were spread across sections. ([TechCrunch, Aug 15, 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/15/tiktoks-new-guidelines-add-subtle-changes-for-live-creators-ai-content-and-more/))
- Prior version (released April 17, 2024, effective May 17, 2024; superseded Sept 13, 2025; read via a mirror copy of the official page): label content "completely generated or significantly edited by AI and contains realistic-appearing scenes or people," either with the AIGC label "or by adding a clear caption, watermark, or sticker of your own"; no AIGC using adult private figures' likeness "without their permission"; no AIGC falsely showing a public figure "taking a position on a political issue, commercial product, or a matter of public importance"; "We do not allow the trade or marketing of services that attempt to artificially increase engagement"; "you should only post your own work"; For You ineligible: "unoriginal or reproduced content that does not have any new or creative changes." ([TikTok Community Guidelines, Integrity and Authenticity, 2024 version](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity), read via [mirror](https://proxy-dev.blitzz.co/proxy/123456/www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity))
- Nov 19, 2025: TikTok said it requires people to "label realistic AI-generated content"; it had labeled over 1.3 billion videos; it began testing an AIGC control in "Manage topics" letting users choose how much AI content appears in For You, and an invisible watermark added to content made with TikTok AI tools (such as AI Editor Pro) and to uploads carrying C2PA Content Credentials. ([TikTok Newsroom, Nov 19, 2025](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/more-ways-to-spot-shape-and-understand-ai-content?lang=en))

## 4. YouTube: altered or synthetic content disclosure, July 2025 YPP "inauthentic content" update, Shopping affiliate rules

### Takeaway
YouTube requires disclosure ("AI use" setting) whenever content meaningfully alters or generates photorealistic content, and penalizes creators who consistently fail to disclose. Since July 15, 2025 it demonetizes "mass-produced and repetitious" (inauthentic) content. Shopping affiliate links may not be used off YouTube. Since Sept 2026 it runs automated systems to catch undeclared promotions.

### Cited Findings
- March 18, 2024: YouTube began requiring creators to disclose realistic content "made with altered or synthetic media, including generative AI." Disclosure examples: using a realistic person's likeness (face swap, or "synthetically generating a person's voice to narrate a video"), altering footage of real events or places, generating realistic scenes. Exempt: productivity uses (scripts, ideas, captions), clearly unrealistic or animated content, color and lighting adjustments, beauty filters. Labels go in the expanded description, with a more prominent player label for sensitive topics (health, news, elections, finance); YouTube may add labels itself; it said it would "look at enforcement measures" for consistent non-disclosure. ([YouTube Official Blog, Mar 18, 2024](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content))
- Current help page ("Disclosing use of GenAI content," accessed Oct 13, 2026): disclose when you "meaningfully alter or generate photorealistic content," whether fully or partly AI-made; set "AI use" in the Attributes section of YouTube Studio; YouTube may auto-label content made with its GenAI tools, content with C2PA metadata, or content its systems detect; creators who consistently don't disclose "may be subject to manual application of a label, or penalties from YouTube," including removal or YPP suspension; disclosure itself does not limit reach or monetization; not required for script, thumbnail or title help, or cloning one's own voice for voiceovers or dubs. ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491))
- July 15, 2025 YPP update: "YouTube has always required creators to upload 'original' and 'authentic' content"; the update helps "better identify mass-produced and repetitious content" and "better reflects what 'inauthentic' content looks like today." ([YouTube policy changelog](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10008196?hl=en#zippy=%2Ccommunity-guidelines))
- The update does not ban such content from YouTube; affected videos can be demonetized or the creator removed from YPP. ([Plagiarism Today, July 8, 2025](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2025/07/08/youtube-targets-inauthentic-content/)) YouTube "hasn't provided an exhaustive list"; reporting expected templated, highly repetitive formats "especially in Shorts" and automated AI channels with "generic voiceovers" to be most affected (reporter's framing). ([Gulf News, July 9, 2025](https://gulfnews.com/technology/youtube-updates-monetisation-policies-ai-and-repetitive-content-ban-begins-july-15-1.500192660))
- Changelog entries, 2025 to 2026: Jan 2025, misleading metadata that "promises viewers something that the video doesn't deliver"; Mar 2025, YouTube "may conduct additional reviews on new video uploads"; June 2026, using YouTube tools "to create new or modified content that harms or is intended to harm others" is a serious violation; Aug 2026, "Promote users running campaigns with AI content can use self-disclosure tools to label their content"; Sept 2026, "transitioning from 'Paid Product Placements' to 'Branded Content Policy'" and "rolling out internal automated systems to identify undeclared promotional material," which may add a viewer disclosure label. ([YouTube policy changelog](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10008196?hl=en#zippy=%2Ccommunity-guidelines))
- Paid promotion: creators with branded content, sponsorships, endorsements or other commercial relationships must "let YouTube know by selecting the paid promotion button in your video details," which shows a label at the start of the video; creators and partners are responsible for legal requirements (FTC named); the page does not mention affiliate links. ([YouTube Help, branded content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235))
- Shopping affiliate program (accessed Oct 13, 2026): US creators in YPP; excludes music channels and Official Artist Channels and Made for Kids channels; routing non-YouTube traffic through Shopping affiliate links violates the Terms ("syndicating links externally, automated redirects, click injection, and cookie stuffing" are examples) and can lead to suspension of affiliate features; commissions paid through AdSense "within 60 to 120 days after purchase"; creators with branded content must follow branded content policies; the page does not say YouTube adds an automatic affiliate disclosure. ([YouTube Help, Shopping affiliate program](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13376398?hl=en))

### Inferences
- A photoreal AI presenter or AI-generated scene requires the "AI use" disclosure. A non-human-sounding or generic AI narration over real product footage is a gray zone: the current page centers on making "a real person appear to say or do something," while the 2024 announcement listed synthetic voice narration under likeness. Disclosing costs nothing in reach (per YouTube), so disclose.
- Treat every affiliate or commission video as "paid promotion" in YouTube Studio plus an in-video disclosure; the Sept 2026 automated detection of undeclared promotions makes silent affiliate content a growing risk. Attorney check is not needed for this; it is a conservative platform choice.
- A faceless channel that publishes many near-identical AI Shorts (same template, swapped product) is the profile the inauthentic-content rule targets. Each video needs distinct research, footage and commentary.

### Gaps
- The text of YouTube's "Reused content" policy and the new Sept 2026 "Branded Content Policy" page were not retrieved.
- Whether YouTube Shopping product tags show an automatic commission disclosure to viewers is not confirmed.
- The YPP subscriber threshold for Shopping affiliate eligibility is not stated on the page retrieved.

### Inferences
- The main business risk for a compliant faceless operation is not account bans but reach and payout erosion: For You ineligibility, demonetization for templated output, user-side AI filters, and brand clawbacks. Original research, real footage and visible disclosure are the defenses that address all four.
- Undisclosed promotion is moving from a legal risk to an automated platform penalty (YouTube Sept 2026, TikTok flagging), so silent affiliate content will get caught by machines before regulators.
