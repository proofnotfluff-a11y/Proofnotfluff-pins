# Music and audio strategy for automated short-form product demo videos (TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, Facebook Reels), as of October 2026

Labels used inline: [Platform primary] = the platform's own docs; [Platform self-reported] = a platform statistic with no independent audit; [Platform-commissioned] = research paid for by the platform; [Vendor] = a tool or music company with a commercial interest; [Third-party summary] = a secondary site that does not quote a primary source; [Older than 12 months] = dated before Oct 13, 2025.

## 1. Does reusing the same background track on every video hurt reach or retention?

### Takeaway
No platform document says that reusing your own background track lowers reach. The originality rules on Instagram, Facebook and TikTok target accounts that repost other people's content. YouTube's rule targets templated, minimally varied, mass-produced channels, and it governs monetization eligibility, not distribution. A single fixed track is not a policy problem on its own, but it adds to the "same template every time" pattern YouTube describes. No credible data was found on whether audio variety affects reach or retention.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" monetization policy to "inauthentic content" effective July 15, 2025, and said "There is no change to our reused content policy." The policy body is headed "Generic or Repetitive Content" and defines it as "content that looks like it's made with a template." [Platform primary; rename dated July 2025, older than 12 months; page fetched Oct 2026] ([YouTube Help: channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392))
- YouTube does not allow these for monetization: "Similar or repetitive content with low educational value, commentary, narratives, or minimal variation across videos"; "Image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative, commentary, or educational value"; and "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass production." It explicitly allows "Same intro and outro for your videos, but the bulk of your content is different." ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392))
- The YouTube policy page does not mention background music, text-to-speech or AI voice. Its only audio examples cover reused songs, for example "Songs modified to change the pitch or speed, but are otherwise identical to the original song." It also says that creators who use automated tools or templates must still show their creative vision in the final product. ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392))
- Instagram (Adam Mosseri video, reported April 30, 2026): accounts that mostly share content they did not create or did not meaningfully transform will no longer be recommended to non-followers. Mosseri: "If most of what you post to Instagram is someone else's content, your account is no longer going to be recommendable." The policy now covers photos and carousels as well as Reels, and originality is judged "across a month of posting, not post by post." The article does not mention audio, templates or an account repeating its own material. [Secondary news report; current] ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/04/30/new-instagram-policies-target-reposted-content/))
- Facebook (Meta blog post of July 14, 2025, as paraphrased by MediaNama): Meta's systems detect duplicate posts and reduce their distribution. Accounts that repeatedly reuse other creators' content can lose monetization for a period. Meta actioned about 500,000 accounts in the first half of 2025. The announcement does not mention audio or music. Meta's original post could not be fetched. [Secondary paraphrase; older than 12 months] ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2025/07/223-meta-cracks-down-on-unoriginal-facebook-content/))
- TikTok: the Community Guidelines update that took effect September 13, 2025 dropped the consolidated list of content that is ineligible for the For You feed. Those details are now spread across other sections. [Older than 12 months] ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/15/tiktoks-new-guidelines-add-subtle-changes-for-live-creators-ai-content-and-more/)) A third-party guideline archive says the former FYF eligibility exclusion clause "is no longer published." [Third-party archive] ([ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/tiktok/tiktok-community-guidelines/for-you-feed-eligibility-exclusion/))
- Evidence for keeping a consistent sound: TikTok cites Ipsos research ("The Power of You," 2020) finding that brand linkage and recall "increase by over 8x when distinctive brand sounds are leveraged in ads," compared with slogans and logos. [Platform-commissioned; ads context, not organic; older than 12 months] ([TikTok for Business: Evolution of Sound](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1))

### Inferences
- On Instagram, Facebook and TikTok the fixed track carries little policy risk. Those rules ask whose content it is, and ProofNotFluff's screen recordings, scripts, narration and music are all its own.
- Sameness matters most on YouTube. The same template, voice, track and screen layout across 1 to 2 videos a day matches YouTube's "minimal variation" and "generic templates giving the impression of mass production" examples. This rule only affects eligibility for the YouTube Partner Program, not reach. Varying the music is cheap insurance, but the real defense is that each video teaches something different, since YouTube names educational value as a factor.
- A middle path fits both YouTube's "same intro and outro" allowance and the brand-sound research. Keep a short fixed sonic logo (about 1 to 2 seconds) as the brand constant, and vary the bed underneath per video or per product (tempo, key, instrumentation, pattern). Code-generated music makes this nearly free.
- It is unknown whether Meta's duplicate detection compares audio beds across one account's own videos. Varying the audio would lower that risk if it exists, but this is speculation.

### Gaps
- No credible creator or analytics study was found on whether using the same track versus different tracks across an account's videos affects reach or retention on any platform.
- TikTok's current wording on unoriginal content and FYF eligibility could not be checked. TikTok's guideline pages block automated fetching, and the archive no longer shows the clause. Meta's original July 2025 post on unoriginal content could not be fetched either.

## 2. Trending sounds vs original audio, TikTok CML and Meta Sound Collection rules, attaching platform sounds through posting APIs, and reusable "original sounds"

### Takeaway
A business account cannot use trending commercial songs on TikTok, because TikTok tells businesses to use only the Commercial Music Library for commercial activity. Automated posting cannot attach TikTok or YouTube library sounds at all. Instagram is the exception. Since June 1, 2026, the Instagram Audio API can attach royalty-free Meta Sound Collection music, or other users' original sounds from Reels, to a Reel at creation time. Buffer added this to its composer on October 7, 2026, but its API support is undocumented. No credible evidence was found that trending audio outperforms original voice-over audio for educational or product-demo accounts.

### Cited Findings
- TikTok Commercial Music Library: "pre-cleared global music library of 1 million songs." "Businesses should instead use the Commercial Music Library for all commercial TikTok activities." "Businesses cannot use the general music library for commercial usage." Permitted uses include organic content (duets, stitches, reacts), video ads and branded content. Businesses using other users' original sounds or other licensed sounds should check with legal that they hold a license. The page does not mention API access or the Content Posting API. [Platform primary; last updated July 2025, older than 12 months] ([TikTok Ads Help: Commercial Music Library](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/commercial-music-library))
- TikTok Content Posting API guidelines require a declaration before the publish button: "By posting, you agree to TikTok's Music Usage Confirmation." The wording changes to add the Branded Content Policy when branded content is selected. The commercial-content toggle labels posts "Promotional content" or "Paid partnership." The page does not say the API can add music or sounds. [Platform primary; undated, copyright 2026] ([TikTok for Developers: Content Sharing Guidelines](https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-sharing-guidelines))
- Buffer: "We don't have access to TikTok's audio library." Trending audio on TikTok requires notification publishing and finishing the post in the TikTok app. Effects cannot be added with automatic publishing "due to limitations within the Instagram and TikTok APIs." The exception is Instagram Reels: "You can add a song or original sound from Instagram's audio library to a Reel and still publish it automatically," for a Business or Creator account connected through Facebook. [Vendor, primary for its own product; undated] ([Buffer Help](https://support.buffer.com/article/933-adding-music-stickers-and-other-effects-to-instagram-and-tiktok-posts))
- Buffer changelog, October 7, 2026: "Search and preview Instagram audio in the composer, attach a track to your reel" and "Buffer publishes it with the sound in place." The entry covers only the composer UI. It does not mention the Buffer API or TikTok. [Vendor; current] ([Buffer changelog](https://buffer.com/changelog/music-collaborators-and-more-for-instagram))
- Instagram Platform changelog, June 1, 2026: "Introducing the Instagram Audio API for apps using Facebook Login," which provides "original sounds from Reels and royalty-free music from Meta's Sound Collection." A new `media_audio_type` field reports whether a Reel's audio is `MUSIC` or `ORIGINAL_SOUND`. A separate ads use case finds Sound Collection tracks to replace copyrighted music in a Reel before it runs as an ad. [Platform primary; current] ([Meta for Developers: Instagram Platform changelog](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/instagram-platform/changelog))
- Instagram Audio API documentation: the API "returns audio that has been authorized for third party use," and the selection may differ from the native app. It requires an Instagram Business or Creator account, a connected Facebook Page, and the instagram_basic and instagram_content_publish permissions. It is "not supported on the Instagram API with Instagram Login." A search with no query returns trending audio. The Reel container accepts an `audio_configuration` object with `audio_id`, `audio_volume` (0 to 100) and `video_volume` (0 to 100). Previewing a Reel with attached audio is not supported. [Platform primary; current] ([Meta for Developers: Instagram Audio API](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/content-publishing/audio-api/))
- Agorapulse, July 27, 2026: audio can be attached only to Reels, not to images, carousels or Stories. Tracks cannot be trimmed and always start from the beginning. The catalog is smaller than the native app's. The "Music" tab is Meta's Sound Collection, and the "Original audio" tab shows "user-generated sounds from existing Reels." [Vendor; current] ([Agorapulse Help](https://support.agorapulse.com/en/articles/16020547-how-to-add-audio-to-instagram-reels))
- YouTube's Audio Library "is found exclusively in YouTube Studio." No API route to attach Audio Library tracks was found. [Platform primary] ([YouTube Help: Audio Library](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3376882))
- Trending vs original reach: the only statement found is secondhand. Kontentino reports Mosseri said in a July 2026 Ask Me Anything, about posts without trending audio, "We're not going to punish you for posting content." It gives no link to a recording or transcript and says nothing about a boost. [Vendor blog; secondhand; unverified] ([Kontentino](https://www.kontentino.com/q-and-a/trending-audio-instagram-reach/))
- TikTok-commissioned research: 88% of users say sound is vital to the TikTok experience (Nielsen, July 2020). 50% say music makes TikTok content more uplifting, energizing and engaging (Kantar, January 2021). Brands using TikTok's music-driven, sound-on ad solutions saw a 120% awareness lift (Kantar, 2021). None of these compare trending and original audio. [Platform-commissioned; ads context; older than 12 months] ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1))

### Inferences
- Original audio (narration plus your own music bed, mixed into the file) is the only approach that works the same way on all four platforms through automated posting. Trending sounds would need manual finishing in the TikTok app, and TikTok restricts them for business use anyway.
- The Music Usage Confirmation declaration suggests TikTok puts responsibility for music rights on the poster when content arrives through the API, so audio mixed into the file needs to be clean. Code-generated music is.
- Instagram is now the one platform where an automated pipeline can attach a platform sound. Option A: keep music mixed into the file everywhere, which is the current setup. Option B, for Instagram only: render a voice-only or voice-plus-quiet-bed file and attach a Sound Collection track through `audio_configuration`, using `audio_volume` and `video_volume` to set the balance. Option B needs either Buffer API support for audio (undocumented) or a direct Instagram Graph API integration through Facebook Login, which breaks the single Buffer pipeline. The tracks cannot be trimmed, start from the beginning, and cannot be previewed via the API, so the voice and music balance is harder to control than in a self-mixed file.
- A Reel with Sound Collection music attached through the API would presumably be classed as `MUSIC` rather than `ORIGINAL_SOUND`. Mixing your own bed into the file keeps the audio as an original sound. For a faceless spreadsheet-demo account, a reusable original sound has little practical upside, since few people will reuse a narrated spreadsheet demo as their audio.

### Gaps
- No credible, non-vendor data compares the reach of trending sounds with original voice-over audio for educational or product-demo accounts on any platform.
- Buffer's public API (as opposed to its composer) has no documentation on attaching Instagram audio.
- No information was found on whether the Facebook Reels publishing API can attach audio.
- Unverified: whether a TikTok business account's upload creates a reusable "original sound" that others can use. Also unverified: whether Instagram's older parameter for naming a Reel's original audio (`audio_name`) still exists, since the current Audio API page does not list it.
- Meta Sound Collection's own license terms (for example, whether tracks can be used off Meta) were not fetched. The changelog only calls the music "royalty-free."

## 3. Free music sources that are legally safe for a business on all platforms at $0, Content ID risk, and whether code-generated music is a sound choice

### Takeaway
Code-generated original music is the safest $0 option for a business posting automatically to four platforms. It carries no third-party license, no attribution duty and no Content ID registration. Every free library has a catch. YouTube Audio Library standard tracks are reportedly YouTube-only, and its CC BY tracks need credit. Pixabay tracks can be registered with Content ID after the fact. Free Music Archive NonCommercial and NoDerivatives tracks are off-limits, and its CC BY tracks need credit in the video itself. Incompetech claims recur when credit is missing. Suno's free tier is non-commercial only.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube Audio Library: downloads "won't be claimed by a rights holder through the Content ID system." Only Audio Library music is "known to YouTube to be copyright-safe," and YouTube is not responsible for "royalty-free" music from other sources. Creative Commons tracks require crediting the artist in the video description, and a filter shows tracks where "Attribution not required." Partner Program members can monetize videos that use Audio Library music. The page does not address use outside YouTube and says "YouTube can't give legal guidance." [Platform primary; undated, fetched Oct 2026] ([YouTube Help: Audio Library](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3376882))
- A third-party summary says "Standard-license tracks are limited to YouTube" and "Using these tracks on other social media platforms, websites, or in podcasts violates the terms of service," while "Creative Commons (CC-BY 4.0) tracks may be used on other platforms, apps, and websites as long as the artist is credited." It does not quote or link YouTube's terms. [Third-party summary; undated; unverified against a primary source] ([LicenseOrg](https://licenseorg.com/guide/music-audio/youtube-audio-library))
- Pixabay, as described by a competing paid music library (Thematic, September 10, 2026): tracks are "licensed for commercial use in videos and social media with no attribution required." A track "can get registered with YouTube's Content ID system after the fact," and then "Every video using that track receives a claim simultaneously, including yours." "Pixabay has no direct Content ID integration," so it "cannot communicate clearance to YouTube on your behalf." Disputes are "manual" and "the outcome is not guaranteed." [Vendor and direct competitor of Pixabay; Pixabay's own license page could not be fetched] ([Thematic vs Pixabay](https://hellothematic.com/thematic-vs-pixabay/))
- Free Music Archive: under NonCommercial licenses, "tracks cannot be licensed for use in commercial endeavours, including videos." Every Creative Commons license requires attribution with Title, Author, Source and License, "in the video as well as in any written blurb that accompanies the work." Under NoDerivatives, "syncing a track to visual media is considered making a 'Derivative Work'," so those tracks cannot be used in video. ShareAlike requires releasing your video under the same license. Content ID use "does not conflict with the terms of a Creative Commons license," and FMA "cannot address Content ID claims directly," so flagged users should contact the artist. A paid Tribe of Noise PRO license removes the attribution requirement. [Platform primary for FMA; undated, copyright 2026] ([Free Music Archive: FAQ for Videos](https://freemusicarchive.org/FAQ_For_Videos/))
- Incompetech (Kevin MacLeod): the music is "pre-registered" so that "it stops the baddies and bad data from claiming my music incorrectly." Claims are "probably" caused by missing credit, as in "It's likely someone forgot to add the credit." The fix is to add the credit to the description and dispute. YouTube releases claims within 72 hours, and a bulk-release form exists for channels with many affected videos. [Primary for Incompetech; undated] ([Incompetech: YouTube Content ID](https://incompetech.com/music/royalty-free/youtube-contentid.html))
- Suno: "Songs made on the free plan (not subscribed) are only available for non-commercial use and cannot be monetized." Paid subscribers get commercial-use rights, but "you generally are not considered the owner of the songs, since the output was generated by Suno." Download limits were announced to take effect in 2026, after Suno's late-November 2025 settlement with Warner Music Group. [News report quoting Suno; December 26, 2025] ([Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/magazine/suno-adjusts-ai-music-ownership-terms-after-warner-music-partnership))
- YouTube requires an AI disclosure when AI "Creates music that's the main focus of the video," and lists "AI generated music" among examples that need disclosure. [Platform primary] ([YouTube Help: altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491))

### Inferences
- Ranking for this account (business, four platforms, automated posting, $0 budget, captions written by an agent):
  1. Code-generated original music, the current approach. It is the cleanest legal position: no license, no attribution, no Content ID registration, and it works on every platform. Its weak points are musical quality and sameness, and both can be fixed in code with parameterized variants and better sound design.
  2. YouTube Audio Library CC BY tracks. Usable off YouTube with credit, per a third-party summary, so every caption would need an artist credit. Avoid standard-license Audio Library tracks for TikTok, Instagram and Facebook, since a third-party summary says they are YouTube-only and YouTube's own page is silent.
  3. Pixabay. Commercial use with no attribution, but the risk of late Content ID registration cannot be cleared through Pixabay, and claims hit every video using the track at once. That is a real nuisance for a channel posting daily.
  4. Free Music Archive or Incompetech CC BY tracks. Legal with attribution. FMA wants credit inside the video as well as in the caption, and NonCommercial or NoDerivatives tracks cannot be used at all. Incompetech claims are a known recurring nuisance when the credit is not machine-readable.
  5. Suno free tier. Not usable for a business (non-commercial only).
- Code-generated synth loops are not "AI-generated music" in the generative-model sense, and as a background bed they are not "the main focus of the video." YouTube's AI-music disclosure should therefore not apply. This would change if the shop switched to a generative AI music model.

### Gaps
- Pixabay's own license text and Content ID guidance could not be fetched, so the Pixabay claims rest on a competitor's description.
- No primary-source text was found for YouTube's Audio Library license on use outside YouTube.
- No data was found on Meta Rights Manager or TikTok fingerprinting matches on free-library tracks.
- Incompetech's exact license (commonly reported as CC BY 4.0) was not verified this session.
- Free-tier commercial terms for other AI music tools (Udio, Stable Audio, Mubert, Google tools) were not researched.
- Copyright ownership of music produced by code that an AI agent wrote was not researched, though this matters little for claim risk.

## 4. Loudness and mixing: platform normalization, integrated loudness and true peak targets, voice-to-music level, ducking, mono vs stereo

### Takeaway
YouTube's playback reference is -14 LUFS, and it turns loud content down but does not turn quiet content up. ProofNotFluff's -24 LUFS masters therefore likely play about 10 dB quieter than their neighbors there (inference). Meta normalizes Reels and Stories loudness on the device to a target it has not published. TikTok publishes no specification, and no credible measurement was found. The best-supported numbers are -14 LUFS (YouTube reference) or -16 to -18 LUFS (AES TD1008 for music and speech) for the master, and at least 10 LU of separation between voice and music, with general listeners preferring about 4 LU more than experts.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube's playback reference changed to -14 LUFS, and some services "(including YouTube and TIDAL) don't turn quieter songs up," so quieter material plays quieter than intended. YouTube now uses the same measurement method as TIDAL and Amazon Music. [Mastering-meter vendor's measurement; September 18, 2019; older than 12 months] ([MeterPlugs](https://www.meterplugs.com/blog/2019/09/18/youtube-changes-loudness-reference-to-14-lufs.html))
- AES TD1008 (2021), as summarized by mastering engineer Ian Shepherd: recommended online distribution loudness is "-18 LUFS" for speech and "assorted" content, and "-16 LUFS" for music streams (album normalization puts the loudest songs at -14 LUFS). Most streaming services were still using about -14 LUFS in 2021. [Expert summary of a standards document; October 1, 2021; older than 12 months] ([Production Advice](https://productionadvice.co.uk/td1008/))
- Meta (April 11, 2023): Reels and Stories on Instagram, and Facebook on iOS and Android, use the xHE-AAC codec, whose loudness management brings "the average loudness of all sessions to the same target level" while "managing the dynamic range of each session to fit the playback environment." Processing happens on the device using "loudness metadata." No target value is given, and the post does not say whether quiet content is raised. [Platform primary; older than 12 months] ([Meta Engineering](https://engineering.fb.com/2023/04/11/video-engineering/high-quality-audio-xhe-aac-codec-meta/)); also summarized by [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/meta-updates-audio-encoding-for-reels-and-stories-to-provide-more-volume-co/647414)
- Torcoli, Freke-Morin, Paulus, Simon and Shirley, "Background ducking to produce esthetically pleasing audio for TV with clear speech" (AES 146th Convention, Dublin, March 20 to 23, 2019; best peer-reviewed paper award). The study recommends at least 10 LU between commentary and music and at least 15 LU between commentary and ambience. "On average, non-experts prefer LDs that are 4 LU higher than the ones preferred by experts." Preferences were highly personal (22 normal-hearing listeners). [Peer-reviewed conference paper; TV context; older than 12 months] ([University of Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1371938/background-ducking-to-produce-esthetically-pleasing-audio-for-tv-with-clear-speech))
- TikTok: no official loudness specification and no credible measurement were found. Search results were SEO and vendor pages with no stated methodology, so they were not used.

### Inferences
- The current -24 LUFS integrated master is 10 LU below YouTube's -14 LUFS reference. Because YouTube does not raise quiet content, these Shorts will likely play noticeably quieter than other Shorts, assuming Shorts uses the same normalization as long-form YouTube (not verified). On Instagram and Facebook, device-side normalization may raise the level, but the target and the amount of gain are unpublished. On TikTok, assume the file plays at its own level.
- Recommended master: about -14 LUFS integrated, with true peak at or below -1 dBTP. The true-peak ceiling is standard practice that was not verified from a primary source this session. A -14 LUFS master is loud enough on platforms that do not raise quiet audio, and platforms that do normalize will simply turn it down.
- Voice-to-music balance: keep music at least 10 LU below the narration while the voice is speaking (Torcoli's floor). For a general audience on phone speakers, aim for roughly 14 to 18 LU of separation, which is Torcoli's floor plus the roughly 4 LU extra that non-experts prefer. Replace the constant low music level with sidechain ducking: music sits low under speech and comes up a few dB in pauses and on visual reveals. This keeps speech clear while letting the music mark structure. The amount of recovery is a judgment call, not a sourced figure.
- Mono vs stereo: keep the narration dead center (mono) and make sure the synth bed sums to mono without phase cancellation. Many phone playback situations are effectively single-speaker. This is engineering practice, not sourced here.

### Gaps
- No published normalization targets were found for TikTok, Instagram or Facebook, and no credible, method-disclosed measurements of their playback loudness were found.
- AES TD1008's true-peak recommendation could not be extracted (the summary table is an image).
- YouTube Shorts-specific normalization behavior was not verified.
- No credible source was found on what share of short-form viewers listen in mono, or on how each platform downmixes stereo.

## 5. Sound effects (typing clicks, whooshes, reveal pops) and music that changes with the story or lands on beats: evidence on retention for explainer or demo videos

### Takeaway
No credible evidence was found that sound effects or beat-synced editing improve retention for explainer or demo videos. Platforms encourage audio of every kind, and Meta lists sound effects among the options, but they cite no data. The best adjacent evidence is TikTok-commissioned research saying distinctive brand sounds strongly improve ad recall. That supports a consistent "signature" sound, not effects in general.

### Cited Findings
- Meta for Developers (November 7, 2024), under "Build with audio": "Include audio to connect with your audience - whether that's voiceover, music, ASMR or sound effects." No data or source is attached. [Platform guidance; older than 12 months] ([Meta for Developers blog](https://developers.facebook.com/blog/post/2024/11/07/unlock-the-power-of-reel-ads/))
- TikTok cites Ipsos (2020): brand linkage and recall "increase by over 8x when distinctive brand sounds are leveraged in ads," compared with slogans and logos. [Platform-commissioned; ads context; older than 12 months] ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1))
- A peer-reviewed study, "The Sound of Reels: Effects of Audio Features of Destination Short Videos on User Engagement" (Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research, published online January 22, 2026), measured audio features such as chroma and zero-crossing rate against engagement in destination-marketing short videos. Only the aims and methods were accessible, not the results. [Peer-reviewed; current; results not seen] ([Metatoc listing](https://metatoc.com/papers/199889-the-sound-of-reels-effects-of-audio-features-of-destination-short-videos-on-user-engagement))
- The Torcoli et al. (2019) ducking study (see question 4) supports varying music level dynamically around speech rather than holding it constant. [Older than 12 months] ([University of Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1371938/background-ducking-to-produce-esthetically-pleasing-audio-for-tv-with-clear-speech))

### Inferences
- Sound effects are nearly free to generate in code and match natural events in a spreadsheet demo: a cell filling, a formula resolving, the final number appearing. Without evidence, treat them as a test variable. Use them sparingly (a payoff "reveal" sound plus light UI ticks) rather than on every action, and compare retention between paired videos with and without them.
- A single consistent reveal sound or sonic logo could double as the brand sound the Ipsos research favors, while the bed underneath varies (see question 1).

### Gaps
- No platform, academic or credible analytics data was found on sound effects, beat-synced edits or story-following music and retention or watch time for explainer or product-demo videos.
- The results of the 2026 "Sound of Reels" study could not be accessed.

## 6. Share of viewers watching with sound on, per platform

### Takeaway
The available platform figures are self-reported and dated. Instagram says over 75% of Reels views are sound-on (2024), and Google says 95% of YouTube ads are audible (2016 data). TikTok only has survey data on attitudes, such as 88% of users saying sound is vital (2020). No current figures were found for Facebook Reels, YouTube Shorts or TikTok viewing behavior. Most plays are audible, so audio quality matters, and captions still serve the muted minority.

### Cited Findings
- Instagram: "Over 75% of Reels views on Instagram are sound on." No source is given. [Platform self-reported; November 7, 2024; older than 12 months] ([Meta for Developers blog](https://developers.facebook.com/blog/post/2024/11/07/unlock-the-power-of-reel-ads/))
- YouTube: "Ads on YouTube are also 95% audible." Footnote: "Google Internal Data, Global, August 2016 (when volume is at least 10% for YouTube ads)." This covers ads, not Shorts or organic videos. [Platform self-reported; post dated March 20, 2017; far older than 12 months] ([Google blog](https://blog.google/products/ads/attention-please-supporting-ad-industry/))
- TikTok: 88% of users say sound is vital to the TikTok experience (Nielsen, "Marketing Science US Authenticity Study," July 2020), and "9 in 10 users" agree sound is vital (Kantar). These measure attitudes, not the share of views with sound on. [Platform-commissioned; older than 12 months] ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1))
- A global EssenceMediacom study on "the changing role of video across Meta" is reported by Campaign, but the figures are behind a sign-in and were not accessible. ([Campaign](https://www.campaignlive.co.uk/article/delivering-impact-rise-reels/1875392))

### Inferences
- On Instagram, roughly a quarter of Reels views may be muted (by Meta's own figure), so burned-in captions remain essential. For the other three quarters, narration clarity and loudness (question 4) directly shape the experience.
- On TikTok, sound-on viewing is likely the norm given the product's design and the attitudinal data, but no behavioral figure supports that.

### Gaps

## 7. Do TikTok, YouTube or Meta require an AI label on videos narrated by a text-to-speech voice that is not a real person's likeness?

### Takeaway
None of the three platforms clearly requires a label for a generic text-to-speech narrator that does not imitate a real person. Meta's rule is the broadest: it requires disclosure for organic content with "realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered" and may penalize non-disclosure. A natural-sounding TTS narrator on Instagram or Facebook is therefore the strongest case for applying a label. TikTok's current guideline targets AI that realistically depicts "people or scenes." YouTube's targets photorealistic content and real people, and it exempts cloning your own voice for voiceovers.

### Cited Findings
- TikTok Community Guidelines (version dated September 24, 2026, as archived by ConductAtlas): "We require clear labeling when AI or editing is used to realistically depict people or scenes." The excerpt does not mention voice, audio or text-to-speech. TikTok's own help page on AI-generated content could not be fetched. [Third-party archive of platform text; current] ([ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/tiktok/tiktok-community-guidelines/ai-generated-content-labeling-requirement/))
- YouTube requires disclosure when creators "use AI to meaningfully alter or generate photorealistic content" that "Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do," "Alters footage of a real event or place," "Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur," or "Creates music that's the main focus of the video." Disclosure is not required for non-realistic content, minor aesthetic edits including "Caption creation," "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs," "voice or audio repair," or generating a script or title with AI. Text-to-speech narration is not listed either way. YouTube "may automatically apply an AI label" to content made with YouTube's generative AI tools, content with C2PA metadata, or content its systems detect as AI-generated or altered. [Platform primary; undated, fetched Oct 2026] ([YouTube Help: altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491))
- Meta: "We'll require people to use this disclosure and label tool when they post organic content with a photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered," and Meta "may apply penalties if they fail to do so." [Platform primary; published February 6, 2024, modified April 1, 2025; older than 12 months] ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/))

### Inferences
- YouTube: no label is needed for a TTS narrator who is not a real person, speaking over a spreadsheet screen recording. Nothing is photorealistic synthetic imagery, no real person is impersonated, and the code-generated music bed is not "the main focus." If the TTS tool embeds C2PA metadata, YouTube may apply a label automatically anyway.
- TikTok: a spreadsheet demo with an off-screen synthetic narrator does not "realistically depict people or scenes," so the current text does not appear to require a label.
- Meta: the literal wording ("realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created") covers a natural-sounding TTS narrator, even though the policy's evident purpose is deceptive media. Applying the AI label to Instagram and Facebook Reels is the conservative choice. Whether the label can be set through the Instagram Graph API or Buffer is unknown.
- The low-risk default is to label on Meta (and optionally everywhere) and to say plainly in the bio or captions that narration is AI-voiced. This also fits the shop's "proof, not fluff" positioning. No evidence was found on whether AI labels reduce reach.

### Gaps
- Not verified: whether TikTok's Content Posting API, the Instagram Graph API, the YouTube Data API or Buffer's API expose a field to mark content as AI-generated.
- No platform statement or data was found on whether an AI label changes distribution.
- TikTok's help-center page on AI-generated content labels and its auto-labeling via Content Credentials could not be fetched.
