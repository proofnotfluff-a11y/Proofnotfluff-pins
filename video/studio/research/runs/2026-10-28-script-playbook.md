# ProofNotFluff Script Playbook: daily 45 to 75 second trade lessons

Research date: Wed Oct 28, 2026 (Central). Built for the video-studio scriptwriter and the facts and golden gates. It sits on top of `video/studio/_shared/voice.md` and `video/studio/research/KNOWLEDGE.md`; where this file and voice.md disagree, voice.md wins until Todd changes it.

## How to read the labels

- **[PLATFORM]** a platform or its executive said it (Instagram, Meta, YouTube, TikTok, ElevenLabs docs).
- **[STUDY]** peer reviewed paper or a large named dataset.
- **[VENDOR]** a company with something to sell published it; treat as directional.
- **[PRACTICE]** what a creator or coach says works; no data behind it.
- **[OURS]** our judgment from the evidence. Best guesses are marked "best guess".
- **[PRIOR RUN]** found in the studio's research run `video/studio/research/runs/2026-10-28-viral.md` or the Oct 15 notes in `research_notes/Short video formulas small business niche/`; not re-read today.

What I could not do: Instagram, TikTok and YouTube block automated reads, so I could not pull raw transcripts of specific shorts. Creator structure below comes from published frame-by-frame breakdowns, creator interviews and the creators' own book or podcast material. No view counts are invented; the few that appear are quoted from the named page.

## 1. How top educational shorts are built (2025 to 2026)

### 1.1 The shared skeleton

Almost every working framework reduces to the same three moves.

- **Hormozi's "content unit": Hook, Retain, Reward.** In $100M Leads (ch. 6) a hook is a topic plus a headline plus a format; a headline should carry at least two of seven elements (recency, relevancy, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing). Retain uses lists, steps or stories, with stories rated most engaging because they leave questions open. Reward means meeting or beating what the hook promised. Longer pieces are several units chained together. [PRACTICE] (Margin Notes summary of ch. 6)
- **Selfstorming's Hook, Retain, Reward write-up** says the same thing in platform terms: hook in 1 to 3 seconds, a middle that gives "a reason to keep watching" every few seconds, and a close where the payoff comes before the CTA. It warns that a flat explanatory middle loses people around 5 to 8 seconds in. [PRACTICE]
- **Jake Van Clief's studio method (our gold standard)**: script and voice first, time every word, then storyboard against the words and draw the scene as code (`video/studio/CLAUDE.md`). A separate Oct 2026 write-up of the same voice-first workflow (ElevenLabs plus Whisper word timing) found the voice sets the pace (a 60 second plan ran 67 seconds), numbers had to be written out in words, and on-screen technical terms read badly when spoken. [PRACTICE] (wwagner.net)

### 1.3 The beats, in order

1. **Hook (0 to 3 s).** The proposition goes in the first 3 seconds and the hook within 6 (TikTok creative best practices) [PLATFORM]. Instagram measures it directly: Reels insights report how many viewers stay past the first 3 seconds, and Instagram said "the first few seconds of reels are the most important" (TechCrunch, Jan 2025) [PLATFORM, PRIOR RUN]. YouTube reports "viewed vs swiped away" [PLATFORM]. Paddy Galloway's study of 5,400 Shorts found Shorts under 60% viewed rarely did well and the best sat at 70 to 90% [VENDOR/PRACTICE, 2023]. One vendor set of 1,111 winning TikToks put the median opening beat at 4.1 s, so the question must be set fast but the hook does not have to be one frantic second [PRIOR RUN, VENDOR].
2. **Stakes (by about 10 to 12 s).** One specific, sourced number or consequence that makes the problem theirs. Hormozi's "conflict" and "relevancy" elements live here.
3. **Say what they will get, early.** Abdaal's Shorts land the promise by 3 to 6 s. Stating the payoff early is not a spoiler problem: one study found readers liked stories more when told the ending first, though later work disagrees (see section 2).
4. **Teaching beats.** One idea per beat, each with a visual change. Agency guidance puts pattern interrupts at about 25 to 40% and 60 to 75% of runtime [PRACTICE, PRIOR RUN]. A "turn" line ("here's the part people skip") works as a spoken interrupt.
5. **Open loops.** Keep them small and close each one inside the video. Hormozi's retain step and Loewenstein's information gap idea both rest on an unanswered question [PRACTICE]. The Upworthy evidence (section 2) says a teaser must still carry enough concrete information.
6. **The copyable artifact.** The exact text, formula or quote line on screen long enough to screenshot. Best guess: this drives saves [OURS].
7. **The end.** Payoff first, then one short CTA. Abdaal ends on the payoff frame. Instagram says sends matter most for reaching non-followers, so the CTA is a send line to a named role, not "follow for more" [PLATFORM]. A last line that echoes the hook makes a clean loop; no published data proves the lift [PRIOR RUN].

## 2. Retention evidence: what predicts watch-through and sends

| Factor | What the evidence says | Label | What we do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ranking signals | Mosseri, Jan 22, 2025: "The top three signals... are watch time, likes and sends." Sends count slightly more for non-followers. July 2024: "One of the most important signals we use in ranking is sends per reach," with the advice to make content people "would want to send to a friend" but "don't force it." | PLATFORM | Every script has one natural send line to a named role. |
| How people actually watch | 9.2 million donated TikTok views from 347 users: 45% watched to the end, 24% skipped before 20% of the video played, 40% left before halfway. Videos from accounts the viewer did not follow were finished slightly more often (44 to 46% vs 38 to 42%). | STUDY (Zannettou et al., CHI 2024) | Assume most people leave; put the payoff before the halfway point and give the leavers something by then. |
| Talking speed | 6.9 million edX sessions: within a length band, faster speakers held viewers up to 2x longer; the authors credit energy, not speed alone, and say to cut filler and pauses rather than slow down. A 2026 review of 12 learning studies: up to about 1.75x normal speed generally fine, above 2x hurts; one study tied 172 wpm to higher credibility. | STUDY (Guo, Kim, Rubin 2014; CoDAS scoping review 2026) | Keep 160 to 195 wpm (voice.md). Cut dead air, not words. |
| Second-person address | 49,782 TikTok news videos: more second-person camera shots meant higher like, comment and share ratios. | STUDY (Cheng and Li, 2024) | Say "you" and talk to one owner. Keep the host facing camera. |
| Conversational style | Meta-analysis: conversational instructional text (second person, direct address) improved retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.54); effects faded in lessons over 35 minutes. | STUDY (Ginns, Martin, Marsh 2013) | Talk like a friend, not a manual. Contractions, "you", one aside. |
| Curiosity gaps | 8,977 Upworthy headline tests: vague teasers win only when the alternatives are too vague; in about 51% of tests the baseline was already too concrete, where more concreteness hurt; in about 9% it was too vague. The best headlines carry "just the right amount" of information. | STUDY (Aubin Le Quere and Matias, Scientific Reports 2025) | A hook names the topic and their stake concretely, and withholds only the answer. "Your quote went quiet? Send this." not "You won't believe this text." |
| Specific numbers | Sharp numbers (99.44%) read as more believable than round ones in ads; one summary puts it at about 10% more believable. | STUDY via secondary summary (Schindler and Yalch 2006) | Use the exact sourced number. Never round a 57% to "most". |
| Payoff early vs late | Readers preferred stories with the ending given first (3 experiments, 12 stories); a later study found spoilers reduced enjoyment. Mixed. | STUDY (Leavitt and Christenfeld 2011; conflicting later work) | Promise the payoff early, deliver it by 70% of runtime. Do not build a whole video around a late reveal. |

| Length | 2026 brand Reels dataset: 45 to 60 s had the highest median views; under 30 s the lowest. Correlational. | VENDOR [PRIOR RUN] | 45 to 60 s is the default; 75 s only when a worked example earns it. |
| Comments | Not in Mosseri's top three. Facebook demotes posts that goad likes, shares or comments; posts that ask for help, advice or recommendations are exempt. | PLATFORM | No "comment YES". Ask a real question whose answers become a future lesson. |

## 3. Hooks

### 3.1 Rules first

- Spoken hook: 9 to 12 words, about the viewer, one concrete stake (voice.md; question hooks and 9 to 12 word hooks did best in one vendor dataset of 14,424 videos [PRIOR RUN, VENDOR]).
- Concrete beats coy (Upworthy result above). Name the job, the number or the mistake.
- Use at least two of Hormozi's headline elements; for us usually **relevancy** (their quotes, their prices) plus **conflict** or **unusual** (a gap, a myth, a surprise in the math).
- Meta's three Reels hook types: value promise, statement of intent, question or invitation [PRIOR RUN, PLATFORM].
- vidIQ's three-part opener: visual hook (show the payoff), text hook of 3 to 7 words, verbal hook of 5 to 10 words, all saying the same thing [PRACTICE].
- Never a greeting, logo or "in this video".

### 3.3 On-screen text hook rules

1. 5 to 8 words, one idea, the same idea as the spoken hook (vidIQ: text and voice reinforce one message) [PRACTICE].
2. Up by 1.0 s, held 2.5 to 4 s; reading floor about 0.3 s per word [PRIOR RUN].
3. Digits on screen ("50%"), words in the voice ("fifty percent").
4. Inside the safe area: out of the top 14% and bottom 35%, about 6% side margins. These are third-party readings of Meta's Reels overlay, not a Meta page I could read; check in Ads Manager's safe zone preview [VENDOR].
5. Text overlays at no more than 5 to 10 words per second overall (TikTok) [PLATFORM].
6. The host and a scene stay on screen: Instagram avoids recommending "mostly text" Reels [PLATFORM, PRIOR RUN].
7. The source rides small on screen whenever a number is shown.

## 4. Writing for the AI voice (ElevenLabs v4 with Todd's clone)

### 4.1 What ElevenLabs says (v3 and v4)

- **Audio tags** go in square brackets right before the clause they shape; the model "carries that tone across the line" when the sentence starts with the emotion. One emotion per sentence segment: contrasting directions "may lead to a less accurate output." To fix a line, swap the tag before rewriting the text. (ElevenLabs blog, Sep 28, 2026) [PLATFORM]
- Tags should describe **voice and delivery**, not actions or non-vocal things like [music], [standing] or [pacing]. Match tags to the voice's character; tags work best when they match how the voice was trained. Overusing tags causes instability; test before production. (ElevenLabs prompting docs) [PLATFORM]
- **No SSML break tags** on v3 or v4. Use tags, punctuation and sentence structure for pauses. Ellipses add a pause but can add hesitation or nervousness. Dashes also work, but our rule bans em and en dashes, so we use a period, a new sentence or a `[short pause]` tag instead. CAPITALS add emphasis. (ElevenLabs help, prompting docs) [PLATFORM]
- **v4 works best on paragraphs**, not single lines (blog). v4 does **not** support the Style and Speed sliders (v4 docs), so pace has to come from the writing. Lower stability gives more varied delivery; higher stays closer to baseline. For professional clones on v4, start Clone Strength at 0%. (v4 docs) [PLATFORM]
- **Numbers and symbols**: normalization is on by default, but models can misread phone numbers, currencies, dates, times, URLs and unit abbreviations; the fix is to expand them in the text first ("Dr." becomes "Doctor"). (prompting docs) [PLATFORM]
- v3 is not optimized for professional voice clones; ElevenLabs recommends v4 for them. v4 launched Sep 28, 2026, with stackable tags and steadier voice identity on long passages (TechCrunch). [PLATFORM]

### 4.2 What makes narration sound robotic, and the fix

| Cause | Fix | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Every clause at the same energy | One tag where the energy changes; vary sentence length (short, short, medium) | Creatide [PRACTICE]; ElevenLabs |
| Long written sentences with no breath | Under 20 words a sentence, about 8 to 10 on average; split at "and", "which", "because" | Creatide [PRACTICE] |
| Page language ("utilize", "in order to", "it is important to note") | Contractions and plain words: "you'll", "here's", "that's it" | Ginns meta-analysis; Creatide |
| Numbers, symbols, acronyms read wrong | Spell as said: "thirty-seven percent", "fifteen hundred", "point six". Turn acronyms into words ("a heating and cooling trade group" for ACCA) or spell them as letters only after a test take | ElevenLabs docs; wwagner.net |
| One huge generation | One paragraph is one take (voice.md), re-roll a weak take alone | Creatide; voice.md |
| Too many or clashing tags | At most two tags a take, one emotion per sentence, no non-vocal tags | ElevenLabs |
| No landing before the payoff | A one-line beat or `[short pause]` before the key number | voice.md; Galloway "punchy" open |

### 4.3 Line-level rules for our scripts

- Spoken numbers in words; digits only in the brief, facts file and on screen.
- No $, %, /, &, # or abbreviations in the voice text. "Fifteen hundred dollars", "forty percent", "and".
- Quotes the viewer will copy are wrapped in straight double quotes so the voice reads them as speech.
- One CAPS word per take at most, and only on the word you would punch out loud.
- Allowed tags (voice.md): `[curious]`, `[leaning in]`, `[matter-of-fact]`, `[a little surprised]`, `[warmly]`, `[short pause]`, plus `[confident]` and `[calm]` as already used in lesson 1. New tags get one test take before use.
- Read every take aloud once. If you run out of breath, split the sentence.

## 5. Series mechanics that compound

What the platforms now support:
- **YouTube Shorts series**: creators can group Shorts into "seasons and episodes" with sequential playback and custom thumbnails, rolling out from Sep 23, 2026 (YouTube blog) [PLATFORM].
- **YouTube related video link**: each Short can link to one of your own Videos, Shorts or Lives in the player (YouTube Help) [PLATFORM]. Link each lesson to the previous one in its series.
- **Instagram/Facebook "Series"**: in limited test since about June 2026 per TechCrunch, as reported by a secondary source; bundles Reels into a hub with a Watch Next button [VENDOR, unconfirmed]. Do not plan around it until it appears on the account.
- Deloitte expects serialized short-form ("micro-series") revenue to more than double to US$7.8 billion in 2026 and says platforms "may" add series-aware recommendations [STUDY/forecast]. That is about dramas, but it shows the direction.
- Gaps in posting can hurt reach, and Mosseri reportedly advises posting again within a day or two after a hit [PLATFORM via secondary, PRIOR RUN]. Electrician U: consistency is what makes you memorable [PRACTICE].

How we build the series (OURS, from the evidence above):
1. **Fixed show frame, changing number.** Like "Fixing 10 Businesses in 10 Days ($150K...)", every lesson title is "[Segment]: [specific claim with a number]". The number is the hook; the frame is the brand.
2. **Four recurring segments**, rotated so no topic runs two days in a row (Todd's mix rule in the video-studio SKILL):
   - **The Text**: one copyable message (follow-up, first reply, review ask).
   - **Run the Numbers**: one piece of math on screen (markup, margin, break-even, ticket).
   - **Proof Check**: one popular stat tested against its source (uses the do-not-use list).
   - **Quote Clinic**: one line or structure on a quote, before and after (fictional, labeled).
3. **Number every lesson** on screen ("Lesson 7") and in the caption. Do not speak the number; it wastes hook time.
4. **Callbacks.** When a lesson builds on an earlier one, one sentence names it ("Same math as the markup lesson."). Link it with the YouTube related video. Callbacks never replace the stand-alone payoff: every episode must work for a stranger.
5. **"Part 2" only when real.** If a lesson truly needs two parts, part 1 still pays off fully and ends with one line naming what part 2 answers. No cliffhanger that withholds the core lesson (it breaks Reward).
6. **Season = a theme of 10.** Group YouTube seasons by theme (follow-up, pricing, reviews) so the playlist reads as a course.

Comment prompts that are not cheap (Facebook exempts asking for advice or recommendations; it demotes goading likes, shares, comments and tags):
- Ask for their real material, and promise to use it: "Tell me the text you send now. I'll rewrite a few in a future lesson."
- Ask a fork they genuinely split on: "Do you put three prices on a quote or one? Tell me why." Use the answers as the hook for a later Proof Check.
- Never: "comment YES", "tag a friend who", "like if", "follow for part 2", or keyword-for-link prompts we cannot deliver.
- Send lines name a role with a reason: "Send this to whoever writes your quotes." One per video, after the payoff. Mosseri's own caution is "don't force it", so the send line is one sentence, never a plea.

## 6. Deliverables

### 6a. One-page script template

```
LESSON: [Segment]: [claim with a number]         Lesson N   Length: 45 | 60 | 75
ONE IDEA (15 words max): ________________________________
WHO SENDS IT TO WHOM: [viewer] -> [role: whoever writes quotes / sets prices / answers the phone]
FACTS (each with source and URL in facts.json): F1 ____  F2 ____  F3 ____

ON-SCREEN HOOK (5 to 8 words, by 1.0 s, held 2.5 to 4 s): ______________

TAKE 1  HOOK          [tag] 9 to 12 words, "you", one concrete stake. Ends on a promise ("Send this.")
TAKE 2  STAKES        [tag] One sourced number (F1) and what it means for them.
TAKE 3  TEACH 1       [tag] The why, in one idea. Small open loop closes inside the take.
TAKE 4  TEACH 2 / ARTIFACT  [tag] The exact text, formula or line. Shown and held 3 s+.
TAKE 5  TURN          [short pause] "Here's the part people skip." A second fact (F2) or the catch.
TAKE 6  (75 s only)   Worked example or how-often / when-to-send.
TAKE 7  CLOSE         [confident] Rule in one line. Send line. Last words echo the hook.
CAPTION: AI voice note, sources, the copyable text, one question for comments.
```

Timing per beat (seconds; at 160 to 195 wpm):

| Beat | 45 s (about 120 to 145 words) | 60 s (about 160 to 195 words) | 75 s (about 200 to 240 words) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0 to 3 | 0 to 3 | 0 to 3 |
| Stakes (F1) | 3 to 10 | 3 to 12 | 3 to 14 |
| Teach 1 | 10 to 20 | 12 to 24 | 14 to 28 |
| Interrupt 1 (visual or turn line), about 30% | about 14 | about 18 | about 23 |
| Teach 2 / artifact on screen | 20 to 33 | 24 to 40 | 28 to 46 |
| Turn (F2 or the catch), about 65% | 29 to 36 | 38 to 47 | 46 to 57 |
| Worked example / cadence | (cut) | (fold into turn) | 57 to 67 |
| Close: rule, send line, loop | 36 to 45 | 47 to 60 | 67 to 75 |

The payoff (the artifact) is fully delivered by about 70% of runtime in every length.

### 6b. The 12-point script checklist (gate scorable)

Each line scores PASS or FAIL with the metric, e.g. `3 artifact: PASS held 3.4 s`.

| # | Check | PASS when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spoken hook | Take 1 opens with 9 to 12 words, contains "you/your" or a trade noun, a concrete stake, and no greeting; ends by 3.0 s in words.json |
| 2 | On-screen hook | 5 to 8 words, same idea as the spoken hook, on screen by 1.0 s, held 2.5 to 4.0 s, inside the safe area |
| 3 | One idea | brief.md states the lesson in 15 words or fewer, and every take serves it (no second lesson) |
| 4 | Stakes early | A sourced number or concrete consequence is spoken by 12 s (45 and 60 s) or 14 s (75 s) |
| 5 | Facts | Every number and named study in voice, screen and caption maps to facts.json with a URL re-read today; spoken form matches the digits; nothing from the do-not-use list; scope words match the source ("home jobs", "HVAC contractors", "on Fiverr") |
| 6 | Payoff timing | What they will get is named by 6 s; the artifact or core answer is complete by 70% of runtime |
| 7 | Copyable artifact | One exact text, formula or line is both spoken and on screen, held 3 s or more |
| 8 | Pace and length | Each take 160 to 195 wpm; total words in the band for the length (45 s: 120 to 145; 60 s: 160 to 195; 75 s: 200 to 240); no sentence over 20 words; average under 12 |
| 9 | Talk test | At least one contraction and one "you" per take; zero words from the banned list (utilize, leverage, game-changer, unlock, secret, hack); zero em or en dashes |
| 10 | Voice markup | At most 2 tags per take, all from the allowed list, none non-vocal; at most 1 CAPS word per take; no digits, $, %, / or & in voice text; no SSML |
| 11 | Retention beats | A visual change every 2 to 4 s in cues.md, plus a spoken turn line between 55% and 75% of runtime |
| 12 | Ending | Exactly one CTA, a send line naming a role, last sentence echoes the hook; no "like/follow/comment YES/tag"; lesson number and segment on screen; caption carries the AI voice note and sources |

### 6c. Three example scripts in our voice

Notes for all three: names in sample texts are placeholders, shown on screen as "example". Each paragraph is one take. Measured with a word counter on Oct 28: no sentence over 20 words, no digits in voice text, no em or en dashes.

#### Script 1. The Text: the text that revives a quote that went quiet

- Segment and length: The Text, 75 s slot (209 words, about 64 to 78 s at 160 to 195 wpm)
- On-screen hook: **"Quote went quiet? Send this text."**
- Artifact on screen: `Hi Dana, it's Mike. Still want that water heater swapped before winter?` (example)
- Send line: "Send this to whoever follows up on your quotes."

```
[leaning in] Your quote went quiet? Don't send "just checking in." Send this.

[matter-of-fact] ServiceTitan says only thirty-seven percent of estimates close on the first visit. So most of your quotes are still open when you pull out of the driveway.

[curious] Here's the problem with "just checking in." It hands them homework. They have to write you an answer, and a busy homeowner puts homework off. The fix is an old trick from marketer Dean Jackson: one short question about what they wanted, and no pitch.

[warmly] So here's the text. "Hi Dana, it's Mike. Still want that water heater swapped before winter?" That's it. Their name, your name, the job, and one question they can answer with a yes or a no.

[short pause] Here's the part people skip. Keep it in your own words. A study in Management Science this year found quick replies stopped helping once people thought a bot wrote them.

[matter-of-fact] When do you send it? Jobber suggests a day or two after the quote. Then about a week later. Then once more, right before it expires. Still nothing after three? Close the file.

[confident] One name, one job, one question. Send this to whoever follows up on your quotes. And next time a quote goes quiet, you know what to send.
```

#### Script 2. Run the Numbers: markup vs margin, why a 50% markup is not 50% profit

- Segment and length: Run the Numbers, 60 s slot (173 words, about 53 to 65 s)
- On-screen hook: **"50% markup is NOT 50% margin"**
- Artifact on screen: `Price = Cost ÷ (1 - margin)` and `40% margin: Cost ÷ 0.6` (a plain hyphen-minus, never a dash character)
- Send line: "Send this to whoever sets your prices."

```
[leaning in] You mark up a job fifty percent. Do you keep half? [short pause] Nope. You keep a third.

[matter-of-fact] Here's the math. Your cost is a thousand dollars. A fifty percent markup adds five hundred, so the quote says fifteen hundred. That five hundred is one third of the price. So your margin is thirty-three percent, not fifty.

[curious] Why the gap? Markup is measured on your cost. Margin is measured on the price, the money the customer actually pays you. And margin is the part of every dollar that's left to pay your overhead and yourself.

[a little surprised] Want a real fifty percent margin? You have to DOUBLE your cost. That's a hundred percent markup. A thousand in cost, two thousand on the quote.

[warmly] Here's the shortcut. Screenshot it. Price equals your cost, divided by one minus the margin you want. Want forty percent? Divide your cost by point six.

[confident] Markup is on cost. Margin is on price. Send this to whoever sets your prices. Then pull up your last quote. Did you keep half, or a third?
```

Facts and sources:

| Line | Fact | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1,000 cost, 50% markup, $1,500 price, 33% margin | Worked example: 50% markup on $1,000 gives $1,500; the $500 is one third of the price | JLC, Sal Alfano, "Markup vs. Margin" (undated page; prior run dates it Dec 2014) | HIGH (arithmetic) |
| Formulas | Margin = (Revenue minus COGS) / Revenue; Markup = (Revenue minus COGS) / COGS; 50% markup gives 33.3% margin | Xero glossary, "Margin vs markup" | HIGH |
| 50% margin needs 100% markup | $1,000 doubled to $2,000 gives 50% margin | JLC | HIGH |
| Price = cost / (1 minus margin); 40% means divide by 0.6 | Rearranged from Xero's margin formula; check: $1,000 / 0.6 = $1,666.67, margin $666.67 / $1,666.67 = 40% | Derived from Xero | HIGH (arithmetic, verified) |
| "left to pay your overhead and yourself" | Gross margin is revenue minus direct cost, before overhead | Xero formula (revenue minus COGS) | HIGH; the script says margin, not net profit, on purpose |

Caption question: "Do you price with markup or margin? Tell me which and why."

#### Script 3. Quote Clinic: three prices on every quote

- Segment and length: Quote Clinic, 75 s slot (201 words, about 62 to 75 s)
- On-screen hook: **"Top shops quote 3 prices. Not 1."**
- Artifact on screen: a three-column quote card: Good (job done right) / Better (what you'd pick for your own house) / Best (plus upgrades: longer warranty, faster start).
- Send line: "Send this to whoever writes your quotes."

```
[leaning in] One price on your quotes? The top shops give three, more often than not.

[matter-of-fact] ServiceTitan looked at its customers' jobs in the year up to this January. The top ten percent offered good, better, best on fifty-seven percent of home jobs. Everyone else? Forty-one.

[curious] Why would more prices help? Researchers studied seven years of orders at a restaurant in Germany. Sometimes the menu added a dish at one end of the price range. The dish that landed in the middle gained about five points of share, on average. And the jump was biggest when the new dish was the expensive one.

[warmly] So build your three like this. Good is the job done right, nothing extra. Better is the one you'd pick for your own house. Best adds the upgrades, like a longer warranty or a faster start date.

[a little surprised] And three is the floor. A heating and cooling trade group surveyed more than a thousand contractors. The ones offering four or more options closed fifty-two percent of the time. One to three options? Forty-two.

[confident] One price is a yes or a no. Three prices is a choice. Send this to whoever writes your quotes, and put three prices on the next one.
```

Facts and sources:

| Line | Fact | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top 10% use good-better-best on 57% of residential jobs vs 41% for others | "Top performers" = 90th percentile of revenue in their trade and market; trailing 12 months through January 2026; aggregated ServiceTitan customer data, business count not stated | ServiceTitan, State of the Trades, Top Performer Profile | MEDIUM (vendor data, correlational: it does not prove options cause the revenue) |
| "more often than not" | 57% is more than half | Same | Safe wording |
| Restaurant study | 7 years, 88,000+ choices at a German restaurant; menu changes turned existing dishes into compromise options, which gained about 5 percentage points of share on average; larger when the added option was at the high end | Pinger, Ruhmer-Krell, Schumacher, Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization 128 (2016) 14 to 34 | HIGH (field data, peer reviewed); the effect ranged from zero to large by category, so "on average" must stay |
| "four or more options closed fifty-two percent... one to three, forty-two" | ACCA and Farmington Consulting Group "Contractor of the Future" survey, more than 1,000 contractors; only 10% offer four or more options | ACHR News, Feb 10, 2026; ACCA blog, Dec 10, 2025 | MEDIUM (survey, correlational; HVACR sample, so the script says "heating and cooling trade group") |
| Good / Better / Best descriptions | Our advice, not a finding | OURS | Opinion, worded as advice |

Honesty note for the gate: the ACCA data does not separate one option from three, so the script never claims three beats one on close rate. It claims (a) top shops use three more often and (b) more options went with higher close rates in that survey, then calls three "the floor".

Caption question: "One price or three on your quotes? Tell me what happens when you give three."
